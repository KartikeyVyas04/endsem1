    Session 11

    Stress at Work

    What stress is, where it comes from, what it does to people and performance, and how individuals and organisations can manage it.

    11.1Why stress at work matters

    Definition

    Stress is a person's response to demands placed on them. Whether that response helps or harms depends on how intense the demand is, how long it lasts, and how well the person can cope.

    Explanation

    Stress is not a rare problem. The American Psychological Association's Stress in America survey (2015) asked people which area of life causes them the most stress. Work came second, just behind money, and ahead of family and health. Because people spend most of their waking hours at work, the way work is designed has a large effect on their well-being.

    The causes of stress also differ across cultures. American employees are stressed mostly by a lack of control over their work. Chinese employees are stressed mostly by job evaluations and a lack of training. The damage, however, is the same in every culture: stress is equally bad for employees everywhere.

    Key points

    Area causing most stress (APA, 2015)% of respondents
    Financial worries64%
    Work60%
    Family responsibilities47%
    Health concerns46%
    Example

    An American software engineer feels stressed because a manager dictates every detail of how code must be written (lack of control). A Chinese colleague on the same team feels stressed because the quarterly rating decides the bonus and no training was given for a new tool (evaluation and training).

    Exam tip

    Quote "work 60%, second only to money at 64%" to open any stress answer. Mention the cultural difference as a moderator.

    11.2Demand → appraisal → response

    Definition

    Stress unfolds in three steps: a demand creates pressure, the person appraises (interprets) it, and that appraisal decides the response.

    Demand
    task, deadline, conflict, uncertainty, change, responsibility    →Appraisal
    challenge, opportunity, threat, or overload?    →Response
    energy and focus, or anxiety, exhaustion, withdrawal, lower performance

    Explanation

    The key idea is the middle step. The demand itself does not decide whether stress is good or bad. What matters is how the person reads it. If they see the demand as a challenge or an opportunity, stress gives energy and focus. If they see it as a threat or as overload, the same demand produces anxiety, exhaustion and withdrawal.

    This explains the slide's message that "not all pressure is harmful". The manager's job is therefore to create demanding work without creating chronic overload: keep the challenge, but make sure people have the time, skills and support to see it as manageable.

    Example

    Two analysts get the same two-week deadline for a client report. Riya has done three similar reports and her manager is supportive, so she reads it as a chance to shine and works with focus. Arjun is new, has never used the data tool, and is also caring for a sick parent, so he reads it as a threat. He sleeps badly and makes mistakes. Same demand, different appraisal, different response.

    Exam tip

    Whenever a question asks "Is stress always bad?", answer with this model: no, it depends on appraisal, intensity, duration and coping.

    11.3Model of stress: sources, moderators, consequences

    Definition

    The stress model shows three groups of potential sources passing through individual differences (moderators) to become experienced stress, which then produces three groups of consequences.

    Explanation

    Potential sources are called "potential" because they only cause stress if the person actually experiences them as stressful.

    • Environmental factors come from outside the organisation: economic uncertainty (recession, layoffs in the industry), political uncertainty (policy changes, elections), and technological change (AI making skills obsolete).
    • Organisational factors come from the job itself: task demands (workload, pace, working conditions), role demands (role conflict, role overload, role ambiguity), and interpersonal demands (lack of support, poor relationships with colleagues or boss).
    • Personal factors come from the employee's life outside work: family problems (marriage, children, elderly parents) and economic problems (debt, EMIs).

    Individual differences act as filters. They explain why two people exposed to the same source feel different levels of stress:

    • Perception: one person sees a restructuring as a threat, another as an opportunity.
    • Job experience: experienced employees have learned coping methods.
    • Social support: supportive colleagues, family or friends buffer stress.
    • Personality traits: for example, people high in neuroticism tend to feel more stress.
    • Cultural differences also shape what is felt as stressful (see 11.1).

    Consequences fall into three groups:

    PhysiologicalPsychologicalBehavioural
    Immediate effects (heart rate, breathing, headaches)
    Illness
    Chronic health conditions (blood pressure, heart disease)    		 Anxiety
    Lower emotional well-being
    Lower job satisfaction (the most obvious sign)    		 Lower job performance and productivity
    Higher absenteeism and turnover
    Changed eating habits, more smoking or alcohol

    Example

    During an industry-wide slowdown (environmental), a sales manager is given a target 40% higher than last year (task demand) and is unclear whether she reports to the regional or national head (role ambiguity). She is also paying a home loan (personal). She has little support at work (moderator). Within months she has frequent headaches (physiological), feels constantly anxious (psychological) and starts taking sick days (behavioural).

    Exam tip

    Draw the model as three columns joined by arrows. Give one example under every heading. This single diagram earns most of the marks for a "sources and consequences" question.

    11.4Stress and performance: the inverted-U

    Definition

    The relationship between stress and performance is an inverted U: performance rises as stress moves from low to moderate, peaks, and then falls as stress becomes high.

    Explanation

    With too little stress, people are bored and under-stimulated. With a moderate amount, they are alert and focused, so performance is highest. Beyond that point, stress starts to exceed coping ability and performance drops.

    Because low-to-moderate stress can be functional, managers may not worry about it. The slide warns of a perception gap: what management sees as "a positive stimulus that keeps the adrenaline running" is very likely to be seen by the employee as "excessive pressure". Managers are often further up the curve than they think.

    Example

    A student with no exam pressure doesn't study (left side). A week before the exam, moderate pressure makes them study efficiently (peak). If three exams fall on one day and they have not slept, panic sets in and they blank out (right side).

    Beyond the slides

    This curve is often called the Yerkes-Dodson law.

    11.5Eustress vs distress

    Definition

    Eustress is positive stress: stress felt as a challenge. Distress is negative stress: persistent stress that exceeds a person's capacity to cope or adapt.

    Key points

    Eustress (positive)Distress (negative)
    Experienced asA challenge, not a threatMore than one can cope with
    ProducesExcitement, motivation, fulfilmentAnxiety, withdrawal, depressive behaviour
    Supports / leads toSelf-efficacy, productivity, innovationBurnout, declining performance
    More likely whenDemands are manageable and meaningfulDemands are excessive; insecurity, conflict, poor conditions

    Explanation

    The line between the two is personal. The same demand can be eustress for one employee and distress for another. A startup's 60-hour weeks may energise a 24-year-old founder-minded employee and crush a colleague with a newborn. Time also matters: eustress that goes on too long without recovery turns into distress.

    Example

    A consultant is chosen to lead a pitch to a big client. She has two weeks, a good team and her partner's trust: eustress, and she does her best work. Six months later she has led four pitches back-to-back with no break and her team has shrunk: the same kind of demand is now distress, and she is close to burnout.

    Exam tip

    Write the sentence "The same demand can be eustress for one employee and distress for another", then explain it using appraisal (11.2) and moderators (11.3).

    11.7EVLN model: how employees respond to dissatisfaction

    Definition

    The EVLN model sorts employee responses to dissatisfaction on two dimensions, active vs passive and constructive vs destructive, giving four responses: Exit, Voice, Loyalty, Neglect.

    ActivePassive
    ConstructiveVoice: actively try to improve the situation. Raise concerns, suggest solutions, seek organisational change.Loyalty: wait patiently and stay supportive, expecting conditions to improve. Stay committed, give the organisation time, defend it.
    DestructiveExit: actively leave or seek alternatives. Resign, transfer, look for another job.Neglect: passively let conditions get worse. Reduce effort, disengage, ignore problems, more absenteeism.

    Explanation

    "Active" means the employee does something; "passive" means they wait or withdraw. "Constructive" responses help the organisation; "destructive" ones hurt it. Voice and loyalty let the organisation recover. Exit costs it talent. Neglect is the most hidden and damaging: the person stays but stops trying.

    The practical lesson is that organisations should make voice easy and safe. When voice is blocked, stressed employees drift toward exit or neglect.

    Example

    Four overworked employees in the same team: Neha writes to HR proposing no-meeting Fridays (Voice). Sameer keeps working and tells juniors things will settle after the peak season (Loyalty). Karthik accepts an offer from a competitor (Exit). Pooja starts logging in late, does the minimum and stops attending optional meetings (Neglect).

    Exam tip

    Draw the 2×2 grid with both axes labelled. The most common mistake is swapping Loyalty and Neglect: both are passive, but loyalty is constructive and neglect is destructive.

    11.8Managing stress: individual and organisational approaches

    Explanation

    Stress can be managed at two levels. Individuals can change how they handle demands. Organisations can change the demands themselves, because many causes of stress (task and role demands) are controlled by management.

    Individual approachesOrganisational approaches
    Time-management techniques
    Increased physical exercise
    Relaxation training (meditation, breathing)
    Expanded social support networks    		 Better onboarding and training
    Goal-setting
    Redesigning jobs
    Employee involvement
    Organisational communication
    Employee sabbaticals
    Wellness programmes

    Key organisational strategies explained

    • Goal-setting: clear goals reduce stress as well as motivate. Employees who are committed to their goals and see purpose in their jobs feel less stress, because they know what "done" looks like.
    • Redesigning jobs: giving more responsibility, more meaningful work, more autonomy and more feedback reduces stress. These give employees greater control over their work and make them less dependent on others.
    • Employee involvement: letting people take part in decisions that affect them increases their sense of control.
    • Organisational communication: more formal communication reduces uncertainty by lowering role ambiguity (not knowing what is expected) and role conflict (facing contradictory expectations). Managers can also use communication to shape how employees perceive events, for example explaining a reorganisation as a growth move.
    • Sabbaticals: extended paid or unpaid time off lets employees recover and return refreshed.
    • Wellness programmes: workshops to quit smoking, control alcohol, lose weight, eat better and exercise regularly. They focus on the employee's total physical and mental condition.
    Example

    A bank's loan officers are stressed by unclear targets and constant escalations. The bank sets monthly targets with each officer (goal-setting), lets them approve small loans without the branch manager (job redesign, more control), sends a weekly note on policy changes (communication), and offers a gym subsidy (wellness). Individually, one officer blocks the first hour of the day for planning (time management).

    Exam tip

    Always give both levels. Then say which one matters more: organisational, because it removes the cause instead of only helping people cope.

    11.9Stress vs depression, and managing depression

    Definition

    Stress is a response to a specific demand or situation. Depression is a mental health condition involving persistent changes in mood, thinking and functioning.

    StressDepression
    Response to a specific demand, pressure or situationPersistent changes in mood, thinking and functioning
    Linked to identifiable stressors: deadlines, conflict, uncertaintyMay occur with or without an obvious external trigger
    Can be positive (eustress) or negative (distress)Involves significant emotional and functional difficulties
    Often improves when the stressor is removed or reducedSymptoms can persist even when circumstances improve
    Feeling tense, overwhelmed, worried, irritable, unable to relaxPersistent sadness or emptiness, loss of interest or pleasure, hopelessness, low energy, difficulty concentrating
    Does not necessarily mean a negative mental stateShould not be dismissed as "being stressed" or "having a bad day"

    Managing depression at work

    • Mental health is part of health. Talking about stress, anxiety, burnout or depression should not be stigmatised.
    • Create an environment where employees can ask for help without fear of judgment.
    • Counselling is legitimate support. Encourage use of counsellors, Employee Assistance Programmes (EAPs), psychologists and other qualified professionals.
    • Managers should listen and support, not diagnose.
    Example

    A team member who was always cheerful has seemed flat and withdrawn for two months, even after a tough project ended. The manager does not say "you're just stressed" or try to label it. She asks privately how he is doing, listens, offers flexible hours, and shares the company's confidential EAP counselling number.

    Exam tip

    The clearest single difference: stress usually eases when the stressor is removed; depression can persist even after circumstances improve.

    Communication Faculty pick

    How a message travels, which direction it flows, how group networks differ, which channel to choose, what gets in the way, and how culture changes meaning.

    12.1The communication process

    Definition

    Communication is the transfer and understanding of meaning. The communication process is the sequence of steps from sender to receiver that makes this happen.

    Sender: message to be sent→Encoding→Channel→Receiver: message received→Decoding→Feedback to sender

    Explanation

    • Sender has an idea to share.
    • Encoding: the sender converts the idea into symbols: words, tone, gestures, charts.
    • Channel: the medium that carries the message: email, meeting, phone, notice board.
    • Receiver gets the message.
    • Decoding: the receiver interprets the symbols back into meaning.
    • Feedback: the receiver's response, which tells the sender whether the message was understood.
    • Noise: anything that distorts the message at any stage: jargon, emotions, assumptions, cultural differences, information overload, a bad network line.

    Communication succeeds only when the meaning decoded matches the meaning encoded. Feedback is the only step that lets the sender check this, which is why one-way communication fails so often.

    Example

    A manager emails "Please prioritise the client report." She means "finish it today". The analyst decodes "prioritise" as "do it before your other tasks this week". The noise is an ambiguous word. If the analyst had replied "Will send by Friday, OK?" (feedback), the gap would have been caught at once.

    Exam tip

    Draw the process with noise affecting every step and a feedback arrow back to the sender. Use it to explain any case where "people didn't do what was asked", such as DRW (12.11).

    12.2Direction of communication: downward, upward, lateral

    Definition

    Communication in organisations flows downward (to a lower level), upward (to a higher level) or laterally (among people at the same level).

    Explanation

    Downward communication flows from one level of a group or organisation to a lower level. Leaders use it to assign goals, give instructions, explain policies and procedures, and give feedback. It must explain why a decision was made; people follow a decision more willingly when they understand the reason. Its major limitation is that it is one-way: managers tell but rarely ask for input.

    Upward communication flows to a higher level. Employees use it to give feedback to their bosses, report progress and raise current problems. Because senior managers have little time, the slide gives three rules:

    • Communicate in headlines, not paragraphs.
    • Support your headlines with actionable items.
    • Prepare an agenda so you use your boss's attention well.

    Lateral communication takes place among members of the same work group, among work groups at the same level, among managers at the same level, or among any horizontally equivalent people. It saves time and helps coordination, and it may be formally sanctioned. But it can create dysfunctional conflict when it bypasses the formal chain and managers learn about decisions after the fact.

    DownwardUpwardLateral
    FlowsHigher → lower levelLower → higher levelSame level
    PurposeGoals, instructions, policies, feedbackFeedback, progress, problemsSave time, coordinate
    Watch out forOne-way; must explain whyKeep it short: headlines + actionsCan cause dysfunctional conflict
    Example

    Downward: HR announces a new hybrid-work policy and explains that it is to cut office costs while keeping collaboration days. Upward: a team lead messages the VP: "Project 2 weeks late. Cause: vendor delay. Need: approval for a second vendor by Monday." Lateral: the marketing and sales managers fix launch dates directly between themselves, and the product head is annoyed at not being consulted.

    12.3Small-group networks: chain, wheel, all-channel

    Definition

    Small-group networks are the patterns through which messages move among members of a group.

    Explanation

    • Chain: follows the formal chain of command. Each person passes information only to the next. Found in rigid hierarchies.
    • Wheel: all communication passes through one central person, who acts as the hub. Found in teams with a strong leader.
    • All-channel: every member communicates freely with every other member. Found in self-managed teams.
    CriterionChainWheelAll-channel
    SpeedModerateFastFast
    AccuracyModerateHighModerate
    Emergence of a leaderModerateHighNone
    Member satisfactionModerateLowHigh

    No single network is best. The wheel is fast and accurate because one person controls the flow, but members feel left out, so satisfaction is low. The all-channel network is fast and satisfying because everyone participates, but with many voices accuracy is only moderate and no leader emerges. The chain is middle on everything.

    Example

    During a factory fire, the plant manager acts as the hub and every supervisor reports only to him (wheel: fast, accurate, clear leader). A startup's product team brainstorming new features uses a group chat where anyone can reply to anyone (all-channel: high satisfaction, many ideas). A government file moving from clerk to section officer to under-secretary to joint secretary follows a chain.

    Exam tip

    Memorise the table. For "which network should the team use?", match the goal: accuracy and control → wheel; satisfaction and creativity → all-channel.

    12.4Informal communication: the grapevine, gossip and rumours

    Definition

    The grapevine is the informal communication network in a group or organisation.

    Explanation

    The grapevine exists in every organisation and cannot be shut down. Instead of fighting it, managers can learn from it. The grapevine:

    • gives managers a feel for the morale of the organisation,
    • identifies issues employees consider important,
    • helps managers tap into employee anxieties.

    Rumours spread fastest when a situation is important, ambiguous and anxiety-provoking, for example layoffs, mergers or leadership changes. The best cure is accurate, timely information.

    Four steps for dealing with gossip and rumours

    1. Share the information you have, and the information you don't. If you don't know something people are asking about, say when you will know, and follow up.
    2. Explain, explain, explain. As a manager, discuss what decisions were made, why they were made, and the plan going forward.
    3. Respond to rumours noncommittally, then verify for yourself the truths you can. Gather all sides of the story.
    4. Invite employees to discuss their concerns, ideas, suggestions, thoughts and feelings. Help them turn their thoughts into more objective viewpoints.
    Example

    A rumour spreads that the Pune office will close. The branch head calls a meeting: "No closure decision has been made (step 1). Leadership reviews all locations in March; I will update you by 15 March (step 1). Here is why the review is happening: rent costs rose 30% (step 2). I've heard various stories; I'll check with head office and come back to you (step 3). Tell me what worries you most (step 4)."

    12.5Nonverbal communication

    Definition

    Nonverbal communication is meaning conveyed without words: tone of voice, emphasis, facial expression, body movement, eye contact and physical distance.

    Explanation: intonation changes meaning

    The same sentence means different things depending on which word is stressed. The slide uses "Why don't I take you to dinner tonight?"

    Stressed wordWhat it really means
    Why don't I take you to dinner tonight?I'm trying to find a reason why I shouldn't take you.
    Why don't I take you to dinner tonight?Do you have a problem with me?
    Why don't I take you to dinner tonight?Instead of the guy you were going with.
    Why don't I take you to dinner tonight?Instead of going on your own.
    Why don't I take you to dinner tonight?I was going to take someone else.
    Why don't I take you to dinner tonight?Instead of lunch tomorrow.
    Why don't I take you to dinner tonight?Not tomorrow night.

    Key points

    • Physical distance has meaning. Proper spacing depends on cultural norms. Distance can signal aggressiveness or sexual interest (too close), or disinterest or displeasure with what is being said (too far).
    • Eye contact is important in business. It shows attention and confidence, but constant staring feels aggressive.
    • Looking at body parts or clothes instead of the face can be perceived as sexual harassment.
    Example

    A manager says "Great job on the presentation" while looking at his phone in a flat tone. The words say praise; the nonverbal cues say "I don't care". Employees believe the nonverbal message.

    Exam tip

    When words and nonverbal cues conflict, receivers usually trust the nonverbal cues. Use this to explain why email (no tone) is often misread.

    12.6Choosing a communication channel: channel richness

    Definition

    Channel richness is the amount of information a channel can carry during one communication episode. Rich channels can handle several cues at once, give immediate feedback, and are personal.

    Formal reports, bulletins→Memos, letters→Prerecorded speeches→E-mail→Online discussion groups→Voice mail→Live speeches→Telephone→Video conferences→Face-to-face

    Low channel richness on the left, high on the right (Daft & Noe).

    Explanation

    Face-to-face conversation is the richest channel: you get words, tone, facial expressions and body language, and you can respond instantly. Formal reports and bulletins are the leanest: one-way, impersonal and slow to get a reply.

    The right channel depends on whether the message is routine or nonroutine:

    • Routine messages are straightforward and have minimal ambiguity. A lean channel works well and is efficient.
    • Nonroutine messages are complicated, ambiguous or emotional. Choose oral communication when you need to gauge the receiver's receptivity, i.e. see how they react.
    • Written communication is more reliable for complex and lengthy messages, because the reader can refer back to it.
    Example

    Change in canteen timings → email (routine). Telling an employee their role is being eliminated → face-to-face (nonroutine, emotional; you need to see their reaction). A detailed new travel reimbursement policy → a written document, followed by a short meeting for questions.

    Exam tip

    A common case error is announcing a major change by email. Criticise it using channel richness: the message was nonroutine, so it needed a rich, two-way channel.

    12.7Persuasive communication: automatic vs controlled processing

    Definition

    Automatic processing is a relatively superficial consideration of evidence and information. Controlled processing is a detailed consideration of evidence and information relying on facts, figures and logic.

    Automatic processingControlled processing
    DepthSuperficialDetailed
    Effort and timeLittleNeeds effort and energy
    Relies onSurface cues: a cute jingle, a glamorous photo, a celebrityFacts, figures and logic
    RiskEasily fooled by tricksHarder to fool

    Explanation

    The slide's rule is match your message to your audience. When the audience is interested, informed and has time, they will use controlled processing, so bring evidence and logic. When the audience is busy, uninterested or low on knowledge, they will use automatic processing, so a simple, vivid, emotionally appealing message works better.

    Example

    Pitching a new software tool to the CFO (high interest, will scrutinise) → a business case with cost savings and payback period (controlled). Persuading all 2,000 employees to use the new expense app → a 30-second video with a catchy tagline and one clear benefit (automatic).

    12.9Cultural barriers and high- vs low-context cultures

    Definition

    In high-context cultures, meaning depends heavily on nonverbal cues, shared history and implicit meaning, and communication is indirect. In low-context cultures, meaning is carried mainly by explicit, direct and detailed words.

    Four causes of cultural barriers

    • Semantics: words mean different things to different people.
    • Word connotations: words imply different things in different languages.
    • Tone differences: some cultures expect formal language in business, others informal.
    • Tolerance for conflict and methods of resolving it differ: some cultures confront openly, others avoid direct disagreement.

    The context scale

    LevelStyleCountries
    Highly high-contextExtremely indirect; relies heavily on nonverbal cues, status and hierarchyJapan, China, Saudi Arabia
    Moderately high-contextLayered and relational; strong emphasis on reading between the linesIndia, Brazil, Mexico, UAE
    Middle / mixedBalances implicit social manners with clear verbal delivery, depending on the situationFrance, Spain, Italy
    Moderately low-contextDirect and explicit, but keeps basic conversational nuance and rhetorical styleUK, Australia, Canada
    Highly low-contextStrictly literal; needs absolute clarity, repetition, precise verbal skills and detailed written documentationUSA, Germany

    Explanation

    In a high-context culture, who says something, how they say it and what is left unsaid matter as much as the words. A "yes" may mean "I heard you", not "I agree". In a low-context culture, people say exactly what they mean and expect contracts and written agreements to spell everything out. Misunderstandings happen when each side reads the other using its own rules.

    Example

    An Indian vendor tells a German client, "We will try our best to look into this." In India this is often a polite way of saying "probably not". The German client reads it literally as a commitment and is angry when nothing happens. The fix: the vendor states clearly what can and cannot be done, and the client confirms in writing.

    12.10Kotter's eight-step plan for implementing change (1996)

    Definition

    John Kotter's eight steps are a sequence for leading organisational change. Several of the steps are about communication.

    1. Establish a sense of urgency by creating a compelling reason why change is needed.
    2. Form a coalition with enough power to lead the change.
    3. Create a new vision to direct the change, and strategies for achieving it.
    4. Communicate the vision throughout the organisation.
    5. Empower others to act on the vision by removing barriers to change.
    6. Plan for, create and reward short-term "wins".
    7. Consolidate improvements, reassess changes and make necessary adjustments.
    8. Reinforce the changes by showing the link between new behaviours and organisational success.

    Explanation

    Kotter's point is that change usually fails not because the idea is bad but because the early steps are skipped: people don't see why change is needed, no powerful group backs it, and the vision is announced once and never explained. Steps 1–4 prepare people, steps 5–6 help them act, and steps 7–8 make the change stick.

    Example

    A hospital introducing electronic patient records: shows data on errors from handwritten files (urgency); gets the medical director and senior nurses on board (coalition); "Every patient's history on one screen" (vision); town halls and ward visits (communicate); trains staff and gives tablets (empower); celebrates the first ward to go paperless (short-term win); extends ward by ward, fixing problems (consolidate); links it to fewer errors in the annual review (reinforce).

    Exam tip

    13.1What is FIRO-B?

    Definition

    FIRO-B (Fundamental Interpersonal Relations Orientation – Behaviour), developed by William Schutz in 1958, is a personality assessment that measures how you typically behave toward other people and how you want them to behave toward you.

    Explanation

    Schutz argued that everyone has three basic interpersonal needs: Inclusion, Control and Affection. People differ in how strongly they feel each need, and in whether they mostly give it to others or want it from others. FIRO-B measures both sides.

    FIRO-B helps you understand:

    • how you come across to others, and why this may not be the way you see yourself;
    • how and why conflict develops between well-meaning people;
    • your own needs as you interact with others;
    • how to manage those needs in most interpersonal interactions.
    Example

    Two well-meaning teammates clash. One keeps organising and assigning tasks (high expressed control). The other hates being told what to do (low wanted control). Neither is a bad person; their needs simply don't fit. FIRO-B makes this visible so they can adjust.

    13.2Three needs × two dimensions = six scores

    Definition

    Each need is measured on two dimensions. Expressed (e) is how much you act this way toward others. Wanted (w) is how much you want others to act this way toward you.

    InclusionControlAffection
    ExpressedeI: how much you make an effort to include others in your activities, join and belong to groups, and be with peopleeC: how much you try to control and influence others or situations, organise and direct others, and take responsibilityeA: how much you try to get close to people and engage them personally; how comfortable you are being open with and supportive of others
    WantedwI: how much you want others to include you and invite you to join groups; how much you want to be noticedwC: how comfortable you are working in well-defined situations with clear expectations and instructionswA: how much you want others to be warm toward you, take a personal interest in you, share things with you and encourage you

    Explanation

    The two dimensions can be very different for the same person. Someone may rarely invite others out (low eI) but badly want to be invited (high wI). They will often feel left out, while others think they prefer to be alone. Comparing one person's expressed score with another person's wanted score is how FIRO-B predicts fit and friction.

    Exam tip

    Note that wanted control is defined differently from the others: it is comfort with clear structure and instructions, i.e. willingness to be directed.

    13.3Inclusion

    Definition

    Inclusion is "the need to establish and maintain satisfactory interactions and associations with other people", and "a recognition of the significance and importance of the self and of others as unique individuals."

    Key points

    • Core question: "Am I in or out?"
    • Indicates: the amount of interaction a person wants.
    • The extent of prominence and significance a person seeks.
    • Ultimate realisation: Fame. Underlying need: Significance.

    Explanation

    Inclusion is about belonging and being noticed at the group level. A high-inclusion person wants to be part of things, invited to meetings, known by name. A low-inclusion person is content working alone and may find constant socialising draining.

    Example

    A new joiner who is not added to the team WhatsApp group feels hurt and ignored. This is an inclusion issue: they are asking "Am I in or out?"

    13.4Control

    Definition

    Control is "the degree to which one desires to influence or direct the behaviour of others." It describes behaviour around responsibility, power, influence and decision-making, and "the extent to which a person wants to be seen as competent, decisive and in charge."

    Key points

    • Core question: "How much control do I possess in the relationship?"
    • Indicates: the need for control of the interaction.
    • Driven by: feelings of respect and competence.
    • Ultimate realisation: Power. Underlying need: Competence.

    Explanation

    Control is about who decides and who follows. High expressed control means taking charge, organising and directing. High wanted control means preferring clear instructions and being comfortable when someone else leads.

    Example

    In a group project, one member immediately makes the plan and assigns sections (high eC). Another member is relieved to be told exactly what to do (high wC). They work well together.

    13.5Affection

    Definition

    Affection describes a person's behaviour in forming close, personal relationships with others. It applies mainly to one-to-one interaction and describes how much people need to feel liked and appreciated.

    Key points

    • Core question: "Am I close or far in relationship to you?"
    • Indicates: the depth of interaction.
    • Driven by: feelings of likeability and openness.
    • Ultimate realisation: Love. Underlying need: Likeability.

    Explanation

    Affection decides the degree of openness, warmth and personal connection a person seeks. Inclusion is about being in the group; affection is about being close to a particular person. A person can have high inclusion but low affection: they like being at every event but keep conversations light.

    Example

    A colleague who shares personal stories and asks about your family is expressing affection. A colleague who is friendly at team lunches but never discusses anything personal may have high inclusion but low affection.

    13.8The Johari Window (Joseph Luft & Harry Ingham, 1955)

    Definition

    The Johari Window is a model of self-awareness that divides information about a person into four panes, based on whether it is known or unknown to the self and known or unknown to others.

    Known to selfUnknown to self
    Known to others1. Open area
    Information both you and others know: your name, visible skills, how you behave in meetings.    		2. Blind area
    Others see it, you don't: a habit of interrupting, a tone that sounds rude, a strength you undervalue.
    Unknown to others3. Hidden area
    You know it, others don't: fears, past failures, private ambitions, personal problems.    		4. Unknown area
    Nobody knows it yet: untested talents, unconscious motives, how you would react in a crisis.

    Explanation: how the panes change

    • Feedback (you ask others): moves information from the Blind area into the Open area.
    • Self-disclosure (you tell others): moves information from the Hidden area into the Open area.
    • Shared discovery: through feedback and disclosure together, parts of the Unknown area become Open.
    • Self-discovery: you learn something new about yourself, moving it from Unknown to Hidden, which you can then disclose.

    The goal is to enlarge the Open area. When more is known on both sides, there is more trust, communication is easier, and fewer misunderstandings arise. A large Blind area causes surprises and conflict; a large Hidden area makes a person seem distant.

    Example

    Priya, a new team member, does not realise she dominates meetings (Blind). A colleague tells her kindly after a meeting (feedback) and it becomes Open. Priya shares that she is nervous about client calls because of a bad experience in her last job (self-disclosure: Hidden → Open), so the team pairs her with a senior. During a crisis, she and the team discover she is excellent at calming angry clients (shared discovery: Unknown → Open).

    Exam tip

    Draw the 2×2 grid with "Ask / feedback" along the top and "Tell / self-disclosure" down the side. Link it to FIRO-B: people with high expressed affection disclose more easily, so their Open area grows faster.

    14.5Tuckman's five-stage model of group development

    Definition

    Bruce Tuckman's model says groups pass through five stages: Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing and Adjourning.

    Explanation of each stage

    1. Forming. The team gets acquainted and establishes ground rules. Formalities are preserved and members treat each other as strangers. There is uncertainty about the group's purpose, structure and leadership. Members depend on the leader and test what behaviour is acceptable. The key issues are roles, goals, trust and dependence.

    2. Storming. Members start to communicate their feelings but still see themselves as individuals rather than part of a team. They resist control by group leaders and show hostility. This is the stage of intragroup conflict over who leads and how work is done. Many groups get stuck here.

    3. Norming. People feel part of the team and realise they can achieve their work if they accept other viewpoints. Close relationships and cohesiveness develop, and the group agrees on shared norms. A feeling of team spirit is experienced.

    4. Performing. The team works in an open and trusting atmosphere where flexibility is key and hierarchy is of little importance. There is a climate of open communication, strong cooperation and lots of helping behaviour. Energy goes into the task rather than into getting to know each other.

    5. Adjourning. For temporary groups, the team assesses its work, plans for transitioning roles, and recognises members' contributions. It is a "return to independence". Members may feel pride or sadness.

    FormingStrangers, ground rules, dependenceGive direction; clarify purpose and roles

    NormingCohesion, shared norms, team spiritFacilitate; reinforce norms

    Forming: polite first meeting; everyone agrees to "divide work equally". Storming: two members argue over primary vs secondary data; another feels the self-appointed leader is bossy.     14.6Group decay (McGrew, Bilotta & Deeney, 1999)

    Definition

    Group decay is the decline of a performing group, passing through three stages: de-norming, de-storming and de-forming. It was observed in a software development team.

    Performing→De-norming
    erosion of standards of conduct    →De-storming
    discontent surfaces; cohesiveness falls    →De-forming
    members care little beyond their own self-imposed borders

    Explanation

    Tuckman's model suggests groups keep improving. McGrew and colleagues showed that high-performing groups can also slide backwards. First the agreed standards slip (people start missing meetings, cutting corners). Then open discontent and conflict return. Finally the group breaks apart into individuals who only look after their own piece of work.

    Example

    A software team that shipped great releases for a year gets a new manager and tighter deadlines. Code reviews get skipped (de-norming).     14.7Social identity, ingroups and outgroups

    Definition

    Social Identity Theory (Tajfel & Turner, 1979) says that people derive part of their identity from the groups they belong to, which leads to in-group favouritism and out-group discrimination.

    Explanation

    Social identity theory rests on three mental steps:

    1. Social categorisation: we sort people, including ourselves, into groups (engineers vs marketers, Mumbai vs Delhi).
    2. Social identification: we adopt the identity of our group and feel its successes and failures as our own.
    3. Social comparison: we compare our group with others ("What benefits am I getting?") and want ours to come out ahead.
    Example

    A cricket fan strongly identifies with their team and views rival fans negatively.

    Self-Categorisation Theory (Turner et al., 1987) builds on this. People categorise themselves at different levels depending on the context: as a singular "I" (personal identity) or as a more inclusive "we" (social identity).

    Example

    A person sees themselves as an individual at work, competing for promotion ("I"), but as a team member during a cricket match ("we").

    Ingroups, outgroups and threat

    • Ingroup favouritism occurs when we see members of our group as better than other people.
    • Whenever there is an ingroup, there is by necessity an outgroup. It is sometimes "everyone else", but usually a specific group known to the ingroup's members.
    • Social identity threat is similar to stereotype threat: the fear of being judged. People believe they will be negatively evaluated because of their association with a particular group, and may lose confidence.
    • People hold multiple identities at once. Intersectionality means these identities combine, each with its own power and status advantages or disadvantages.
    Example

    The only woman engineer in a team worries that if she makes a mistake it will be seen as proof that "women aren't good at coding". This anxiety makes her hesitate to speak up. That is social identity threat. If she is also from a small town and the first in her family to attend college, several identities intersect.

    14.9Group conformity and Asch's study

    Definition

    Group conformity is the tendency of individuals to align their attitudes, beliefs and behaviours with those of a group.

    Explanation

    Conformity happens for three reasons given on the slide: social pressure, the desire to fit in, and the belief that the group is more knowledgeable or correct. It can make group decisions uniform, sometimes at the expense of critical thinking.

    Asch's study (Solomon Asch, 1950s)

    • Aim: to find out how far social pressure from a majority can make a person conform.
    • Method: participants matched the length of a line on one card to one of three lines on another card. The right answer was obvious.
    • The other people in the room were confederates of the experimenter who deliberately chose the wrong line, to see if the lone real participant would go along.
    • Result: about 75% of participants conformed to the wrong answer at least once, even when they knew it was wrong.
    Example

    A new joiner thinks a proposed campaign is weak, but when five senior colleagues praise it, she says it looks great too. Later she admits she agreed only because everyone else did.

    Group polarisationGroup discussion leads members to adopt more extreme positionsInvestors who were mildly doubtful about a startup become strongly opposed after discussing it together

    Definition

    Key terms

    • Role perception: one's own view of how one is supposed to act.
    • Role expectations: how others believe one should act in a given situation. Example: people may expect a female leader to be nurturing and a male leader to be assertive.
    • Psychological contract: the unwritten mutual beliefs, perceptions and informal obligations between two parties, such as employer and employee.
    • Role conflict: a situation in which an individual faces divergent role expectations.
    • Interrole conflict: when the expectations of our different, separate groups are in opposition.
    Example

    Psychological contract: an employee works late during a crunch expecting the company to be flexible when they need time off. If the manager later refuses leave, the employee feels the unwritten contract has been broken. Interrole conflict: a manager who is also a parent is expected at a late client call and at their child's school event on the same evening.

    Stanford Prison Experiment (Philip Zimbardo, 1971)

    • Aim: to study the psychological effects of perceived power.
    • Method: 24 male college students were randomly assigned to be "guards" or "prisoners" in a mock prison.
    • Guards enforced rules; prisoners experienced simulated incarceration. Participants quickly adopted their roles: guards became authoritarian, prisoners showed signs of stress.
    • Result: planned for 2 weeks, it was stopped after 6 days because of guards' abusive behaviour and prisoners' emotional breakdowns.
    • Finding: power and social roles shape behaviour, leading to dehumanisation and loss of identity.
    Exam tip

    Use Zimbardo to argue that behaviour at work is shaped by the role and power people are given, not only by their personality.

    14.15Creating team players, when not to use teams, and why cooperation is hard

    Creating team players

    • Selecting: hire people with team skills.
    • Training: build team skills through workshops on problem-solving, communication and conflict.
    • Rewarding: give incentives for cooperation, not just individual performance.

    When not to use teams

    Teams take more time and resources than individuals. Before forming one, ask:

    1. Can the work be done better by one person?
    2. Does the work create a common goal or purpose?
    3. Are the members of the group interdependent?

    If one person can do it better, or there is no common goal, or members don't depend on each other, use individuals instead.

    Compete vs collaborate

    In the class game, competing could give one group higher points, but collaborating increased the total points earned by all groups together.

    Why is cooperation so hard?

    • Goal incongruence: each group pursues its own objectives.
    • Lack of trust: you have no control over the other group's decisions.
    • Uncertainty: you are not sure how the others will behave.
    Example

    Two regional sales teams could share leads and both grow. But each is rewarded only on its own numbers (goal incongruence), neither trusts the other to share good leads (lack of trust), and neither knows if the other will reciprocate (uncertainty). So both hoard leads and the company loses.

    16.2What is leadership?

    Definition

    Leadership is the ability to influence a group toward the achievement of a vision or set of goals.

    Explanation

    • Not all leaders are managers, nor are all managers leaders. A manager holds a formal position with authority. A leader influences people, whether or not they have the title. The best managers are also leaders.
    • Nonsanctioned leadership (the ability to influence that arises outside the formal structure) is often as important as, or more important than, formal influence.
    Example

    In a class project, the group "leader" chosen on paper books rooms and sends reminders. But everyone waits to hear what one respected classmate thinks before agreeing to anything. That classmate is the real, nonsanctioned leader.

    16.3Trait theory: are leaders born?

    Definition

    Trait theories of leadership focus on personal qualities and characteristics that differentiate leaders from non-leaders.

    Explanation

    This is the oldest approach to leadership research. It searched for personality, social, physical or intellectual attributes that great leaders share, on the assumption that leaders are born. Early lists were long and inconsistent. Organising traits around the Big Five personality model gave clearer results:

    • Extraversion, conscientiousness and openness to experience are the traits most related to leadership.
    • People high in emotional intelligence (EI) are more likely to emerge as leaders, even after taking cognitive ability and personality into account.
    Example

    In a new MBA section, the student who speaks up confidently (extraversion), always comes prepared (conscientiousness), suggests fresh ideas (openness) and reads the room well (EI) is often elected class representative.

    Beyond the slides

    Common criticism: traits predict who emerges as a leader better than who is effective, and they ignore the situation. This gap led to behavioural and contingency theories.

    16.4Behavioural theories: leaders are made

    Definition

    Behavioural theories say effective leadership is learned through actions and habits rather than born traits. They study what effective leaders do.

    1. Hawthorne Experiments (Elton Mayo, 1920s–1930s)

    Found that people have social needs. Supervision, workplace conditions and social rewards affect productivity, not just pay and physical conditions.

    2. Ohio State Leadership Studies (1940s)

    • Initiating structure (task-oriented): the extent to which a leader defines and structures their role and those of subordinates to achieve goals: organising work, setting deadlines, assigning tasks.
    • Consideration (people-oriented): the extent to which a leader shows camaraderie, mutual trust, respect for employees' ideas, and regard for their feelings.
    • Key takeaway: the "High-High" style (high on both) achieved the highest subordinate performance and satisfaction.

    3. University of Michigan Leadership Studies (1950s)

    • Production-centred (task-oriented): focuses on the technical aspects of the job, keeping employees busy, meeting production schedules.
    • Employee-centred (people-oriented): focuses on the human aspects of subordinates.
    • Key takeaway: employee-centred leaders achieved significantly higher group productivity and job satisfaction.

    A project manager who sets a clear weekly plan with deadlines (initiating structure) and also checks how team members are coping, listens to their ideas and defends them in front of the client (consideration) is a High-High leader.

    Don't mix up the findings: Ohio State says both dimensions high; Michigan says employee-centred wins.

    16.10Power and dependence

    Definition

    Power is a capacity that X has to influence the behaviour of Y so that Y acts in accordance with X's wishes.

    Explanation

    • Power is a capacity: it may exist without being used.
    • The most important aspect of power is that it is a function of dependence. A person can have power over you only if they control something you desire.

    The general dependency postulate

    When you possess anything that others require but that you alone control, you make them dependent on you, and you gain power over them. Dependence is inversely proportional to the alternative sources of supply: the more alternatives people have, the less they depend on you.

    What creates dependence?

    • Importance: the resource you control must be important to others.
    • Scarcity: the resource must be scarce.
    • Nonsubstitutability: the fewer substitutes for the resource, the more power you have.
    Example

    The only data scientist in a startup who understands the pricing algorithm has a lot of power: the algorithm is important, her skill is scarce, and she cannot easily be replaced. When the company hires two more data scientists and documents the model, her power falls.

    16.12Organisational politics

    Definition

    Political behaviour is activity that is not required as part of a person's formal role in the organisation but that influences, or attempts to influence, the distribution of advantages and disadvantages within the organisation.     Explanation

    Politics is what happens when people use power outside their job descriptions. It is not automatically bad. Building alliances to get a good idea approved is political; so is spreading rumours about a rival. What makes politics harmful is when it serves narrow self-interest at the organisation's expense.

    Example

    Building a relationship with the CEO's chief of staff to get your project on the board agenda; taking credit for a team success; withholding information from a rival department. None is in anyone's job description, but each affects who gains and who loses.

    17.1Crisis leadership

    Definition

    Crisis leadership is leading an organisation through a sudden, high-stakes event, and through recovery afterwards.

    During the crisis

    • The Golden Rule: tell it all, tell it fast, and tell the truth. Silence and half-truths let rumours fill the gap and destroy trust.
    • Internal vs external messaging: tailor separate communication flows for employees, stakeholders and the public. Each group has different questions.
    • Empathy + Action framework: acknowledge that people's anxiety is valid, and clearly outline the immediate next steps.

    Post-crisis recovery and learning

    • After-action review: a blameless post-mortem to evaluate what worked, what failed and why. "Blameless" means the focus is on fixing systems, not punishing individuals, so people speak honestly.
    • Rebuilding trust: restore morale and brand reputation once the immediate threat has passed.
    Example

    A packaged-food company finds contamination in one batch. The CEO announces a recall the same day with batch numbers (tell it all, fast, truthfully). Employees get a separate note on job security and what to tell customers; distributors get logistics instructions; the public gets a press release (internal vs external). The message says "We know parents are worried; here is how to get a full refund today" (empathy + action). Afterwards, a blameless review finds a gap in supplier testing and fixes it.

    17.2Leader-Member Exchange (LMX) theory (Graen, Dansereau & Haga, 1970)

    Definition

    LMX theory says leaders form distinct, individual relationships with each follower, and these relationships shape each follower's performance, job satisfaction and career growth.

    Explanation

    Earlier theories treated a team as one uniform group that the leader treats the same way. LMX recognises that leaders, often early and without meaning to, sort followers into two groups:

    In-groupOut-group
    Quality of exchangeHigh-qualityLower-quality, purely transactional
    What they receiveMore trust, support, challenging assignments, rewards and face-time with the leaderManaged through formal rules, policies and basic job descriptions; no extra perks or deep mentoring
    What they give backHigher loyalty, commitment and performanceWhat the job requires
    Example

    A manager always gives high-visibility client pitches to the two analysts she trusts (in-group). Others get routine tasks and formal reviews (out-group). The in-group learns faster and performs better, which confirms her belief. It becomes a self-fulfilling cycle.

    Exam tip

    Implication: leaders should try to build high-quality relationships with all members, because out-group status lowers satisfaction and feels unfair. Link it to social identity theory (14.7) for extra marks.

    17.4Fiedler's contingency model (Fred Fiedler, 1964)

    Definition

    The Fiedler contingency model says there is no single best way to lead. A leader's effectiveness depends on matching their natural leadership style to the right situation.

    Explanation

    The broader contingency approach says the most effective style depends ("is contingent") on the specific situation and factors in the organisation. Fiedler adds a strong assumption: a leader's style is fixed. So if the style does not fit the situation, you must either change the situation or change the leader.

    Step 1: identify the leader's style (LPC scale)

    The leader fills in the Least Preferred Co-worker (LPC) questionnaire, rating the person they least liked working with.

    • High LPC score (describes even the least-liked co-worker fairly positively) → relationship-oriented leader.
    • Low LPC score (describes that co-worker harshly) → task-oriented leader.

    Step 2: assess situational favourableness

    • Leader-member relations: good or poor. How much members trust, respect and have confidence in the leader.
    • Task structure: high or low. How clear and procedural the job is.
    • Position power: strong or weak. How much formal power the leader has to hire, fire, reward and punish.

    Step 3: match style to situation

    17.5Milgram's obedience experiment (Yale University, 1961–65)

    Definition

    Stanley Milgram's experiments studied obedience to authority: how far ordinary people will go in harming someone when instructed by an authority figure.

    Explanation

    • Background: Milgram grew up with relatives who survived the Holocaust, and wanted to understand how ordinary people took part in atrocities.
    • Cover story: participants believed they were in a study on the effects of punishment on learning.
    • Method: the participant was the "teacher". An authority figure in a lab coat ordered them to give electric shocks to a "learner" (whom they could hear but not see) strapped to a chair. Shocks started at 15 volts and rose after each wrong answer up to 450 volts. The shocks were not real, but participants did not know that.
    • Result: 65% continued to 450 volts in the baseline study.
    • Explanation: obedience to authority overrode participants' ethics, sympathy and moral conduct. This helped explain how atrocities could be committed under the Nazis.
    • Implication: power dynamics and authority can make individuals act against their own moral beliefs.
    Workplace example

    An accountant is told by the CFO to book revenue early to meet quarterly targets. He knows it is wrong, but "the CFO said so". This is the Milgram effect in an office. Ethical leadership and whistle-blower channels are safeguards.

    18.1Conflict by its effect: functional vs dysfunctional

    Definition

    Conflict is a process that begins when one party perceives that another party has negatively affected, or is about to negatively affect, something the first party cares about. Functional conflict supports the group's goals and improves performance. Dysfunctional conflict hinders group performance.

    Explanation

    18.3The Thomas-Kilmann conflict model

    18.4The three class scenarios

    Scenario 1: Priyam and Karan → Compromise

    Priyam and Karan have worked together on several projects. Priyam feels Karan often interrupts her and dismisses her suggestions. Karan believes Priyam gets defensive whenever her ideas are questioned. They now avoid speaking directly and communicate through others. In a meeting, Priyam says "There is no point discussing this with you because you never listen." Karan replies "Maybe the problem is that you expect everyone to agree with you." Their manager wants it resolved before their next project together.

    • Conflict type: relationship conflict (dysfunctional).
    • Why compromise: each has a partly valid point and each must change a little. They must keep working together, so a practical give-and-take works: Karan agrees not to interrupt; Priyam agrees to treat questions about her ideas as not personal.

    Scenario 2: Ananya and Rahul → Collaborate

    A consulting team is advising a consumer-goods company on entering a new market. Ananya wants to launch immediately to capture market share before competitors grow stronger. Rahul wants to delay until more research is done, because the available data is incomplete. Both have evidence. They challenge each other's assumptions and analysis. The team must submit one recommendation to the client the next day.

    • Conflict type: task conflict (functional).
    • Why collaborate: both concerns (first-mover advantage and financial risk) are important and can be combined. For example, a phased or pilot launch in one city while gathering data, with clear go/no-go criteria for full launch.

    Scenario 3: Rohan and Meera → Avoid, Compete or Accommodate?

    Rohan and Meera, both senior managers, are competing for the same regional director promotion. Only one can get it; the decision is next week. Meera believes her performance record is stronger; Rohan believes his recent client acquisition makes him better. Their relationship deteriorates: Rohan highlights Meera's past mistakes to their manager; Meera questions Rohan's figures. They stop sharing information and undermine each other in team meetings. Neither will give up, and both cannot get the job.

    • Conflict type: a fixed, zero-sum outcome that has turned into relationship conflict.
    • Analysis: collaboration and compromise are impossible on the promotion itself, because it cannot be shared or split.
    • Compete fairly on merit, presenting their own records, but stop the sabotage.
    • Avoid the personal fight until the decision is made; let the manager decide on evidence.
    • Accommodate after the decision: the person who loses supports the winner to protect the working relationship.
    • The manager should also step in: make the selection criteria transparent and stop the information-hoarding.
    Exam tip

    For any scenario: (1) name the conflict type, (2) say whether it is functional or dysfunctional, (3) pick a mode, (4) justify it, (5) say what the manager should do.

    18.5Negotiation: distributive vs integrative

    Definition

    Negotiation is strategic bargaining between two or more parties who want to find an acceptable solution to a shared problem. Distributive negotiation divides a fixed amount of resources in a win-lose setup. Integrative negotiation seeks to expand the total value available, for a mutually beneficial win-win outcome.

    DistributiveIntegrative
    ResourcesFixed "pie" to dividePie can be expanded
    OutcomeWin-loseWin-win
    FocusPositions ("I want ₹15 lakh")Interests ("I want growth and security")
    Information sharingLow; hide your limitsHigh; share needs and priorities
    RelationshipShort-termLong-term
    ExampleHaggling over a used car's priceSalary plus joining bonus, remote days and a training budget

    The slide gives the definitions and the win-lose / win-win outcome. The other rows are standard comparison points.

    Example: the orange

    Two chefs both want the last orange. Splitting it 50-50 is distributive: each gets half. If they ask why each wants it, they find one needs the juice and the other needs the peel for a cake. Both can get 100% of what they need. That is integrative negotiation.

    18.6BATNA, reservation point and ZOPA

    Definition

    BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement) is your fallback option if the negotiation fails. The reservation point is the worst deal you are willing to accept. ZOPA (Zone of Potential Agreement) is the shared range, or overlap, where the minimum acceptable terms of the two parties meet.

    Explanation

    • A stronger BATNA gives higher negotiation power, because you can walk away.
    • The outcome depends on the relative BATNAs of the two sides.
    • In a job offer, the candidate's BATNA is other job offers or their current salary. HR's BATNA is the availability of other candidates and its budget limits.

    Worked example: Aditi's salary

    Aditi has an offer from a consulting firm for an MBA graduate position at ₹13 lakh a year. She also has an offer from a smaller consulting firm at ₹12.5 lakh. She prefers the first company for its larger projects and career opportunities, but will not accept below ₹13 lakh. The HR manager's budget is ₹12–15 lakh, but paying more than ₹14.5 lakh would create internal pay inequity, because recent hires earn around that level. The company has two other shortlisted candidates willing to accept ₹13 lakh.

    AditiCompany
    BATNA₹12.5 L: the other offer₹13 L: hire another shortlisted candidate
    Reservation point₹13 L: the minimum she will accept₹14.5 L: the maximum it will realistically pay
    • ZOPA: ₹13–14.5 lakh. Negotiation range: ₹1.5 lakh.
    • Who has more power? The company. Its BATNA (other candidates at ₹13 L) is strong, so it can hold close to ₹13 L. Aditi's BATNA (₹12.5 L) is below her own reservation point, so walking away costs her.
    • How Aditi can do better: strengthen her BATNA (seek a better alternative offer); show unique value so she is less substitutable; and negotiate integratively on non-salary items (joining bonus, early salary review, choice of projects), since salary is capped by internal equity.
    Exam tip

    Always draw the number line. ZOPA runs from the seller's reservation point (here, Aditi's minimum) to the buyer's reservation point (the company's maximum). If the ranges do not overlap, there is no ZOPA and no deal is possible.

    18.7Understanding yourself in negotiation: the Big Five

    Definition

    The Big Five personality traits (extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, openness and neuroticism) each affect how a person negotiates.

    Summary

    • Extroverted negotiators focus on possible solutions; introverted negotiators focus on the root cause of the problem.
    • Agreeable negotiators are generally more successful than disagreeable ones.
    • Conscientiousness matters more than any other trait.
    • Anxious, depressed and worried negotiators underperform.
    • A creative personality (openness) brings more alternative ideas for win-win outcomes.

    Each trait in detail

    Extraversion vs introversion. Extroverts are more influenced than introverts by the opponent's first offer. Introverts bring a valuable skill to the table: listening. They prefer to deliberate privately on important decisions, so they may be less likely to make impulsive, poorly thought-out group decisions.

    Agreeableness. Flexibility, trust and cooperation matter in every successful negotiation. But extreme agreeableness can lead to a lose-win outcome, where you give away too much. Similar pairs reach agreement faster (two highly agreeable negotiators). Disagreeable negotiators do better against other disagreeable negotiators.

    Conscientiousness. Self-discipline, organisation, carefulness and responsibility. This is the trait most closely linked to high negotiation performance. Everyone can behave more conscientiously by taking more time to prepare and improving their organisational skills.

    Openness. Imagination, broad-mindedness and divergent thinking help generate creative solutions by exploring a range of ideas. High-openness negotiators created greater mutual gain in integrative negotiation, but did not perform better in distributive negotiation.

    Neuroticism. A person's general level of anxiety, depression, worry and insecurity. People high on neuroticism tend to get negative outcomes in negotiation.

    Example

    In a salary negotiation, an extroverted candidate may anchor on HR's first number and negotiate around it. An introverted candidate may listen carefully, notice that HR is worried about internal equity, and propose a joining bonus instead of a higher base. That is an integrative solution, helped by listening and openness.

    Exam tip

    If asked "Which trait matters most?", answer conscientiousness, because preparation drives results. Mention that openness helps only in integrative, not distributive, negotiation.
