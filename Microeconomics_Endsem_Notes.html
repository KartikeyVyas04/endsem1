Chapter 1

Production: Turning Inputs into Output

From: Session 11-12 Production & Cost (slides 1–16) · Class Activity 1 & 2 (Part A)

1.1 What is production?

Every firm buys inputs and turns them into output (goods or services). Economists write this as a production function:

Q = f (Land, Labour, Capital, Enterprise)

Each input earns a reward, and that reward is a cost to the firm:

Factor of productionWhat it isReward (cost to the firm)
LandSpace, location, natural resourcesRent
LabourWorkers' time and effortWages / salary
CapitalMachines, equipment, buildingsInterest (EMIs)
EnterpriseThe owner who organises everything and bears riskProfit
Example from class: a tomato farm
Output Y (tonnes of tomatoes) = f (land, fertiliser/seeds/water, capital, labour). The farmer pays rent for land, buys seeds and water, pays interest on the tractor loan and wages to workers. The firm's aim: maximise profit = revenue − cost of production.
Example from class: "Let's open a fresh fruit juice shack near campus"
  1. Land/space: rent and licence (you're stuck with it for the contract period)
  2. Equipment: mixer, juicer, glasses, cookware
  3. Labour: wage paid to a helper (Mohan)
  4. Electricity, water supply, fruit, sugar, ice, straws: these change with how much juice you make
Items 1–2 can't be changed quickly (fixed). Labour and raw materials can be changed daily (variable). That split is the basis of the short run.

1.2 Short run: one factor is fixed

In the short run at least one input (usually capital: the shop, the oven, the looms) is fixed. The only way to raise output is to add more of the variable input, usually labour. So we study how output changes as we add workers to a fixed amount of capital.

1.3 Total, Marginal and Average Product

Total Product (TP)
Total output produced by L workers.
Marginal Product (MP)
MPL = ΔTP / ΔL
Extra output from one more worker.
Average Product (AP)
APL = TP / L
Output per worker.

1.4 Law of Diminishing Marginal Product

Law of Diminishing Marginal Product (DMPL)
When more and more units of a variable input (labour) are added to a fixed input (capital), the marginal product of the variable input eventually falls.

Why does MP rise at first? (1) The fixed machine is better utilised (one worker can't use the mixer, juicer and counter at once). (2) Workers can specialise: one cuts fruit, one runs the juicer, one serves.

Why does MP fall later? Too many workers share the same fixed capital: they wait for the mixer, get in each other's way, and things get inefficient. Each extra worker adds less. Past some point an extra worker reduces output (MP negative): the kitchen is overcrowded.

Discussion answers (the questions from the activity)

  1. When does MP start diminishing while still positive? After the 3rd worker: MP falls from 15 (L=3) to 12 (L=4).
  2. When does MP turn negative? At the 9th worker (MP = −1). The firm should stop hiring: paying a wage for someone who lowers output is pure loss. The kitchen is overcrowded.
  3. Where is AP maximum? At L = 4 (AP = 11.75). Here MP ≈ AP (12 vs 11.75): MP cuts AP at AP's peak.
  4. Which stage would a rational firm never choose? Stage 3 (negative MP). Stage 1 is also not a stopping point, because each extra worker still raises output per worker, so you should keep hiring. A rational firm operates in Stage 2.

Intuition: the class-average trick
Your average marks are 70. If your next test (the "marginal" mark) is 90, your average goes up. If it's 50, it goes down. The same logic applies to MP vs AP, and later to MC vs AC.

1.7 The Three Stages of Production

Elasticity of production EP = (% change in output) / (% change in labour) = MP / AP.

StageFrom → toMP & AP behaviourEPRational?
Stage 1

Common mistake
Diminishing MP does not mean output falls. It means output rises by less each time. Output only falls when MP becomes negative. Also note: the inflection point (MP max) comes before the AP max, which comes before the TP max.

Costs of Production (Short Run)

From: Session 11-12 Production & Cost (slides 11–34) · Class Activity 1 & 2 (Part B) · Economic vs accounting profit illustration

2.1 Fixed vs Variable cost

Fixed Cost (FC)
Does not change with output. Paid even if you produce zero.
Juice shack: rent, equipment, connections/licence.
Variable Cost (VC)
Changes with output. Zero when output is zero.
Juice shack: fruit, sugar, water, ice, straws, electricity, labour.
TC = TFC + TVC

2.2 The two questions Caleb asks

Caleb owns a coffee shop and asks his supervisor two questions that sound similar but are different:

"How much does the typical cup cost?"
→ Average Total Cost
ATC = TC / Q
"How much does one more cup cost?"
→ Marginal Cost
MC = ΔTC / ΔQ
MC(nth unit) = TC(n) − TC(n−1)

Full family of cost measures:

TermDefinitionFormula
Explicit costsCosts requiring an outlay of money—
Implicit costsCosts not requiring a money outlay (opportunity cost)—
Fixed costDo not vary with outputFC
Variable costVary with outputVC
Total costMarket value of all inputs usedTC = FC + VC
Average fixed costFixed cost per unitAFC = FC / Q
Average variable costVariable cost per unitAVC = VC / Q
Average total costTotal cost per unitATC = TC / Q = AFC + AVC
Marginal costIncrease in TC from one extra unitMC = ΔTC / ΔQ
Quick numerical (from the board)
Juice shack: Q = 1000 glasses, TFC = ₹50,000, TVC = ₹10,000.
AFC = 50,000 / 1000 = ₹50 · AVC = 10,000 / 1000 = ₹10 · AC = AFC + AVC = ₹60 per glass.

2.4 Why do the curves have these shapes?

MC and AC rules (very important)
  • When MC < AC, AC is falling.
  • When MC > AC, AC is rising.
  • MC cuts AC (and AVC) at its lowest point: MC = min AC.
  • Marginal cost eventually rises with output (diminishing MP).

The gap between ATC and AVC is AFC. Because AFC shrinks as Q rises, ATC and AVC get closer together, and ATC's minimum is at a higher output than AVC's minimum.

2.6 Accountant vs Economist: two kinds of profit

Explicit costs need an actual money payment (wages, rent, raw material). Implicit costs are opportunity costs with no money payment: e.g. the salary the owner gives up by running the firm instead of taking a job, or interest lost on the owner's own money invested.

Accounting profit = Revenue − Explicit costs
Economic profit = Revenue − Explicit costs − Implicit costs
Normal profit = zero economic profit
If economic profit = 0, the firm is covering all opportunity costs. The owner earns exactly what they could earn elsewhere. Accounting profit is still positive. This is called normal profit, and it's enough to keep the firm in business.

Also, from the slide on profit: Economic profit = Accounting profit − Opportunity cost. Economic profit is always ≤ accounting profit.

2.7 Summary (textbook)

  • A firm's goal is to maximise profit = TR − TC.
  • Include all opportunity costs (explicit + implicit).
  • A typical production function gets flatter as input rises (diminishing MP), so the TC curve gets steeper as output rises.
  • ATC first falls then rises; MC always crosses ATC at ATC's minimum.
  • Many costs are fixed in the short run but variable in the long run (next chapter).
Quick self-test
  1. If ATC = 50 and MC = 40 at some output, is ATC rising or falling? → Falling (MC < ATC).
  2. TFC = ₹600. What is AFC at Q = 30? → ₹20.
  3. TC at 10 units = 250, at 11 units = 268. MC of the 11th unit? → ₹18.
  4. Can economic profit be zero while accounting profit is positive? → Yes. That's normal profit.
Chapter 3

Long-Run Costs & Economies of Scale

From: Long run cost curve.pdf (all slides incl. handwritten notes)

3.1 Short run vs long run

  • Short run: at least one factor is fixed and at least one is variable. E.g. the number and size of factories is fixed, so to increase output the firm can only hire more labour.
  • As we move from short run to long run, more factors (and so more costs) become variable.
  • Long run: all factors and all costs are variable. The firm can open new plants or shut existing ones.

In the long run we plot ATC (per-unit cost) instead of TC on the y-axis: ATC = TC/Q.

3.2 The samosa example

Planning a samosa business
A thela (cart) can optimally serve 100 samosas/day. We haven't started yet and are deciding how many thelas to buy. We're confident of selling 200 samosas/day.
With 1 thela, 100/day costs ₹25 each, and pushing 200 through one thela would be very expensive (overcrowding). With 2 thelas, 200/day costs only ₹15 each. So in the long run we pick the plant size (number of thelas) with the lowest cost for our planned output.

3.3 LRAC = the "envelope" of SRAC curves

For every output level, the firm chooses the plant size (SRAC) that gives the lowest cost. Joining those lowest points gives the Long-Run Average Cost (LRAC) curve. It "wraps" all the SRAC curves from below, so it's called the envelope curve (or planning curve).

RegionWhat happensReturns to scale
LRAC fallingOutput ↑ faster than cost ↑ ("TQ ↑↑↑, TC ↑")Economies of scale / Increasing Returns to Scale (IRS)
LRAC flatOutput and cost ↑ in the same proportionConstant Returns to Scale (CRS)
LRAC risingCost ↑ faster than outputDiseconomies of scale / Decreasing Returns to Scale (DRS)
Textbook example: car factory
Small factory: ATC ≈ $12,000 per car. Medium/large factory around 1,000–1,200 cars/day: ATC ≈ $10,000 (the flat, constant-returns region). A very large factory: cost rises again (diseconomies).

3.4 Minimum Efficient Scale (MES)

Definition
MES is the smallest output level at which long-run AC reaches its minimum, i.e. the point where the firm stops getting economies of scale. Getting bigger after MES "doesn't get better" in cost terms.

Why it matters: if MES is large relative to market demand, only a few firms can survive (oligopoly / natural monopoly). If MES is small, many firms can compete.

3.5 Sources of Economies of Scale

Internal economies (inside the firm, as it grows)
  • Bulk buying: better bargaining power, raw material bought in bulk → lower procurement cost
  • Customer acquisition cost ↓: advertising and marketing get cheaper per customer (reputation, repeat buying)
  • Managerial specialisation: specialist managers → higher efficiency
  • Cheaper financing: big firms borrow at lower interest (lower cost of capital)
  • R&D costs ↓ per unit, risk-bearing capacity ↑
  • Technical upgrades: bigger, more efficient machines; specialisation of labour and machines
  • Cutting out the middleman (better sourcing)
External economies (from the industry/cluster growing)
  • Infrastructure improvement and regulatory support
  • Suppliers move closer to your cluster (lower procurement cost)
  • Pool of specialised, lower-cost labour
  • R&D facilities around your sector
  • Reduced taxes / incentives for the cluster
Think: Bengaluru for IT, Tiruppur for knitwear.

3.6 Diseconomies of Scale

  • Coordination and communication problems in a huge organisation
  • Control and motivation problems (workers feel like a small cog)
  • Managerial inefficiency: task complexity grows
  • May have to pay more for inputs; raw material cost ↑ as you exhaust cheap sources
  • New and more expensive inputs needed
Don't confuse these two
Diminishing marginal product is a short-run idea (one input fixed, you add labour). Diseconomies of scale are a long-run idea (all inputs scaled up together). SRAC is U-shaped because of DMP; LRAC is U-shaped because of economies then diseconomies of scale.
Quick self-test
  1. In the long run, which costs are fixed? → None. All are variable.
  2. LRAC is falling. What does this show? → Economies of scale (IRS).
  3. Define MES. → The smallest output at which LRAC is minimised.
  4. Give one internal and one external economy. → Bulk buying; suppliers clustering nearby.

Market Structures & Perfect Competition

From: Market Structure, Perfect competition.pdf (all 32 slides) · shut down, exit point etc.pdf

4.1 Why market structure matters

Firms try to maximise profit → profit depends on the price they can charge → price depends on the market structure, i.e. the degree of competition between firms in the industry. (An industry = all firms supplying a particular market, e.g. autos, shoes, wheat. "Industry" and "market" are used interchangeably.)

Determining factors: (1) number of firms/sellers, (2) ease of entry & exit, (3) nature of product (identical or differentiated).

We study each structure as Structure → Conduct → Outcome (Performance).

4.2 Features of Perfect Competition

Structure
  • Many buyers and sellers
  • Goods are largely the same (homogeneous)
  • Free entry and exit
  • Information available to all
Conduct
Firms are price-takers: no single firm can influence the price. They sell whatever they want at the market price.
Examples from class
A small dairy farmer in Kheda district: his output is tiny compared to the whole market, so he sells all his milk at the market price. An oil-extracting firm in Venezuela: the global market (S & D) fixes the oil price; the local producer is a price-taker who faces a perfectly elastic demand.

The industry's demand and supply fix P̄. Each firm faces a horizontal demand curve at P̄. If it charges more, it sells nothing. It has no reason to charge less.

4.4 Profit maximisation: MR = MC

Profit π = TR − TC. To maximise, take the derivative w.r.t. Q and set it to 0:

dπ/dQ = dTR/dQ − dTC/dQ = 0 ⇒ MR − MC = 0 ⇒ MR = MC

For a competitive firm MR = P, so the rule becomes P = MC.

The logic in words
While MR > MC, each extra unit adds more to revenue than to cost, so produce more; some gains are still unexploited. Once MC > MR, each extra unit reduces profit, so cut back. Profit peaks where MR = MC (here Q = 5, profit $7).
Exam point

4.5 Measuring profit on the graph

Profit = TR − TC = (TR/Q − TC/Q) × Q = (AR − AC) × Q = (P − AC) × Q

(P − AC) = profit per unit. Multiply by quantity for the total profit rectangle: height = P − AC at Q*, width = Q*.

4.6 Shutdown vs Exit: what should a loss-making firm do?

From: shut down, exit point etc.pdf

Shutdown: a SHORT-RUN decision
Stop producing for now. Fixed costs (rent, machines) still have to be paid. The firm is still in the industry. Shutdown is NOT exit.
Exit: a LONG-RUN decision
Leave the industry for good. In the long run all costs are variable (no fixed cost), so by exiting the firm pays nothing.

Why it matters: TC = TFC + TVC. If the firm shuts down in the short run, TR = 0 and TVC = 0, but it still pays TFC ("burning a lot of cash to keep up the operation"). So the question is: does producing lose less than shutting down?

Short run: shut down if P < AVC

Keep producing if TR ≥ TVC
⇒ TR/Q ≥ TVC/Q
⇒ AR ≥ AVC
⇒ P ≥ AVC (in perfect competition P = AR = MR)

Shut down if P < AVC
  • In the short run, revenue should at least cover the operational (variable) expenses.
  • Out of the surplus of revenue over VC, the firm pays part of its FC.
  • If revenue falls short of VC, it's better not to produce in the short run: producing would lose FC plus part of VC.

Long run: exit if P < ATC

Exit if TR < TC ⇒ TR/Q < TC/Q ⇒ P < ATC
Enter (for an aspiring entrepreneur) if P > ATC

Break-even point

Break-even: TR = TC ⇒ TR/Q = TC/Q ⇒ AR = AC ⇒ Price = ATC (at min ATC)

At break-even the firm earns normal profit (zero economic profit, positive accounting profit). Above this point: supernormal profit, so new producers come in, supply rises and price falls. Below it: subnormal profit (loss).

Price rangeShort-run decisionLong-run decision
P > ATCProduce where P = MC, supernormal profitStay; new firms enter

Worked example (using the curves above, FC = ₹10)
  • P = ₹4.50 (between min AVC 3.19 and min ATC 5.49): produce Q ≈ 4.44 where P = MC. ATC ≈ 5.54, AVC ≈ 3.28.
    If it produces, loss = (5.54 − 4.50) × 4.44 ≈ ₹4.6. If it shuts down, loss = FC = ₹10. → Keep producing in the short run, since the ₹4.6 loss is less than ₹10. In the long run it should exit (P < ATC).
  • P = ₹3.19 (exactly the shutdown point): producing loses exactly ₹10 = FC. The firm is indifferent between producing and shutting down.
  • P below ₹3.19: each unit doesn't even cover its variable cost, so producing loses more than FC. → Shut down.
Intuition from class
Price falls → "If I have to continue in the short run, I have to at least cover my variable cost. If not → shutdown point (and wait for demand to pick up). In the long run, if it continues → EXIT."

4.7 The firm's supply curve = its MC curve

A competitive firm produces where P = MR = MC. So for each price, the MC curve tells you the quantity supplied: the MC curve is the supply curve (P₁ → Q₁, P₂ → Q₂ …). But only the part where the firm actually produces:

  • Short-run supply curve = the part of MC above min AVC, starting at the shutdown point. Below that price the firm supplies zero (it sits on the vertical axis).
  • Long-run supply curve = the part of MC above min (long-run) ATC, starting at the exit point. Below that price the firm exits.
Summary
Break-even: TR = TC (P = ATC) · Shutdown (short run): P < AVC · Exit (long run): TR < TC (P < ATC) · Supply curve = MC above AVC (SR) or above ATC (LR).
Common mistake
Don't say a firm shuts down just because it makes a loss. In the short run a loss-making firm keeps producing as long as P ≥ AVC. Shutting down only makes sense when P can't even cover AVC.

4.8 Market supply: short run and long run

Short run: fixed number of firms

  • Number of firms is fixed, say 1000, and all firms are identical.
  • Each firm supplies as long as P > AVC (along its MC curve).
  • Market supply at any price = firm's output × number of firms.

Long run: free entry and exit

  • Firms can now enter or exit, and everyone has access to the same technology and markets, so existing and potential firms face identical cost curves.
  • If firms make economic profits, new firms enter and push the price back down to zero economic profit. Likewise, losses cause exit and price rises back.
  • So in the long run price always returns to min ATC. The long-run market supply curve is horizontal (perfectly elastic) at min ATC.

4.9 Implications of perfect competition

Implications
  • Industry costs of production are minimised
  • Output produced is optimised
Firms maximise profit by
  • Producing where P = MC
  • Entering if P > AC, exiting if P < AC
  • Balancing production across firms so that total industry cost is minimised for any given quantity (every firm produces where its MC = the same price)
Recollect: why markets work
Markets connect and coordinate the world. Price is a signal wrapped in an incentive. Prices move resources from low-value uses to high-value uses.

4.10 Long run: why profits go to zero

  • Supernormal profit → new firms enter (free entry) → market supply shifts right (S₁ → S₂) → price falls → profits shrink.
  • Losses → some firms exit → supply shifts left → price rises → losses shrink.
  • This continues until P = min ATC: zero economic profit (normal profit) in the long run.
What's so "perfect" about perfect competition?
In long-run equilibrium: P = MC (allocative efficiency, so no deadweight loss and total surplus is maximised) and P = min ATC (productive efficiency: goods are made at the lowest possible cost). Consumers get the lowest sustainable price.

For the shutdown rule and the firm's supply curves, see 4.6–4.8.

Quick self-test

  6. Shutdown point vs exit point? → Shutdown: P = min AVC (short run). Exit: P = min ATC (long run).
  7. What is a competitive firm's short-run supply curve? → Its MC curve above min AVC.
Chapter 5

Monopoly

From: 9. Market structure 2 Monopoly.pdf · Price discrimination easy notes (monopoly cases)

5.1 What is a monopoly?

Structure
A single seller with many buyers; no close substitutes; entry barriers ("gatekeepers").
Conduct
The seller is a price maker. But once it sets the price, the quantity sold is decided by market demand. It can't pick both P and Q.

Examples discussed: Indian Railways, IRCTC (regulated), Google (dominant market share), De Beers (diamonds: owns key resource), government electricity boards.

5.2 Why monopolies arise

  1. Key resource owned by a single firm: complete ownership of a resource (De Beers and diamond mines).
  2. Government grants an exclusive right: patents, copyrights, licences (Indian Railways, IRCTC).
  3. Natural monopoly: the cost structure makes it cheaper for one firm to supply the whole market than several firms. ATC keeps falling over the relevant range of output (huge fixed cost, tiny marginal cost). Examples: electricity distribution, rail tracks, water pipelines. Duplicating them is wasteful.
Real-world cases (easy notes)
Not all monopolies are the same: legal, natural, network, resource, regulated.
  • Indian Railways vs IRCTC: Railways runs trains, tracks, signalling, stations. IRCTC runs ticketing, catering, tourism, Rail Neer.
  • Why IRCTC exists: there's a single inventory of seats. Centralised booking avoids double-booking. It's a high-fixed-cost, very-low-MC platform.
  • Railways vs airlines: railways have one inventory → centralised reservation (monopoly coordination platform). Airlines have many competing inventories → competition, and aggregators like MakeMyTrip compare them.
  • Why Amazon/Flipkart report losses: acquire users, build scale, create network effects, monetise later. They give up current profit for future market power.

A competitive firm faces a horizontal demand curve. A monopolist is the market, so it faces the downward-sloping market demand. To sell more, it must lower the price on all units.

  • AR curve = demand curve (AR = TR/Q = P).

  • MR = 0 where TR is maximum; MR is negative beyond that.
Why MR < P: output effect vs price effect
When output rises from 3 to 4 units, price falls from 8 to 7:
  • Output effect (+): one more unit sold at $7 → +7
  • Price effect (−): the 3 units that used to sell at $8 now sell at $7 → −3
MR = 7 − 3 = 4. MR is positive when the output effect exceeds the price effect, negative otherwise. (For a competitive firm there's no price effect, so MR = P.)

Markup = P − MC. It depends on the elasticity of demand (remember AR is the demand curve):

Conclusion
The less sensitive demand is to price (more inelastic), the higher the markup of price over MC.

Pharmaceuticals: two effects make demand inelastic and raise the markup:

  • "You can't take it with you" effect: people with serious illness are relatively insensitive to the price of medicine.
  • "Other people's money" effect: if a third party (insurer, government, employer) pays, the user is relatively insensitive to price.

5.6 Social cost of monopoly: deadweight loss

The benevolent social planner maximises total surplus. Total surplus is maximised where marginal benefit = marginal cost. Every point on the demand curve shows marginal benefit, so the efficient output is where demand meets MC, i.e. P = MC.

  • A monopolist produces less than the efficient quantity and charges P > MC.
  • Customers who value the good more than MC but less than P don't buy. Mutually beneficial trades don't happen → deadweight loss (DWL).
  • Part of consumer surplus is transferred to the monopolist (not a loss to society, just a transfer); the DWL triangle is a pure loss.
  • The monopolist acts like a "private tax collector".
Pharma example
Drug priced at ₹80, MC = ₹8. Patients willing to pay, say, ₹30 would gladly buy (₹30 > ₹8 cost to society), but don't at ₹80. These "valuable" trades are lost because they don't maximise the monopolist's profit.

5.7 Regulating a natural monopoly

  1. Set P = AC: the firm breaks even (zero economic profit), but output is below the efficient level.
  2. Set P = MC and subsidise the gap between price and AC: efficient output, but taxpayers pay for the subsidy.

5.8 Costs and benefits of monopoly: patents

Many monopolies are a direct result of corruption. These have economic and social costs and hardly any benefits. But some monopolies (patents) serve a purpose:

  • If the government eliminated pharma patents, competition would push the price down to MC and remove the DWL.
  • But bringing a drug to market costs billions in R&D, and R&D is not included in MC. "It costs billions to create the first pill but hardly ₹10 to create the next."
  • At P = MC, firms can't recover R&D → fewer new drugs. The same applies to other high-development, low-MC goods (IT, software, movies). This justifies patents.
  • Policy trade-off: low price today vs fewer new ideas in future.

Alternatives to patents:

  • Patent buy-outs: government buys the patent (paying for expected monopoly profits), then tears it up so competitors drive price to MC. Costs: higher taxes, hard to set the price, rent-seeking and corruption.
  • Prizes: government reimburses R&D, so firms can price at MC.
  • Price discrimination (next chapter).
Quick self-test
  1. Why is MR below demand for a monopolist? → To sell one more unit it must cut price on all units (price effect).
  2. Rule for monopoly output? → MR = MC, then price from the demand curve.
  3. Which has a bigger markup: insulin or chocolates? → Insulin (inelastic demand).
  4. Two ways to regulate a natural monopoly? → P = AC; or P = MC with a subsidy.

Price Discrimination, Tying & Bundling

From: 10. Price Discrimination.pdf · Price discrimination easy notes.pdf

6.1 Core idea

Price discrimination
Selling the same product at different prices to different customers, not because of cost differences but because of differences in willingness to pay. The goal: charge closer to each customer's willingness to pay and convert consumer surplus into producer surplus. "Price discrimination = applied elasticity."

Conditions needed: (1) the firm has market power, (2) customers can be separated into groups with different willingness to pay / elasticity, (3) no arbitrage (no reselling from the cheap market to the expensive one).

6.2 Case study: Nightowl Publishing

The publisher pays a best-selling author $2 million. MC of a book = 0. Two kinds of readers:

  • 100,000 "die-hard" fans (in the US) willing to pay $30
  • 400,000 casual readers (in Australia) willing to pay up to $5
StrategyRevenueProfit (− $2M)
Sell only to die-hards at $30100k × 30 = $3M$1M
Sell to all 500k at $5500k × 5 = $2.5M$0.5M
Price discriminate: $30 in US, $5 in Australia3M + 2M = $5M$3M

It works because the two groups are geographically separate and there's no resale. It also increases output: casual readers get the book too.

6.3 Example 2: GSK's AIDS drug and arbitrage

GSK sold Combivir at $12.50 in Europe and at a much lower price in Africa. Official reason: humanitarian. More realistic: profit maximisation. European demand is more inelastic, African demand more elastic, so each market gets its own MR = MC price.

  • Arbitrage threat: smugglers buy cheap pills in Africa and resell them in Europe below the European price. Price discrimination fails when arbitrage is possible.
  • If a single "world price" is enforced: the price isn't profit-maximising in either market. Most likely GSK sets Europe's monopoly price and withdraws from Africa.

6.4 Welfare effects

  • If price discrimination increases output, welfare improves; if not, welfare falls.
  • GSK: with a single world price, Europeans are just as well off (or better), Africans are worse off (no drug at all). So here price discrimination is beneficial: total surplus is higher due to greater output.
  • Industries with high fixed costs (pharma, airlines) can put extra profits into R&D or more routes. Airlines offer more flights to more destinations at different times.
  • Price discrimination helps spread high fixed costs across a larger population.

6.5 Perfect (first-degree) price discrimination

Left: a single price leaves consumer surplus and a deadweight loss. Right: each customer pays exactly their willingness to pay (each one is a separate "market segment"). The firm sells until P = MC, so output is efficient: CS = 0, DWL = 0, all surplus becomes profit.

The monopolist's dream: 3 students, MC = 0

6.7 Tying

A form of price discrimination where a base good is "tied" to a variable good. Examples: printer + cartridges, Kindle + e-books, gaming console + games.

  • The base good is cheap; the variable good is priced above MC. HP printers are cheap, cartridges are expensive.
  • Buyers are really paying for the ability to print colour photos. High-value users print more, so they pay more per photo than low users. It works like a meter.
  • Tying usually increases welfare by increasing output. Without tying, printers would be expensive and ink cheap: heavy users still buy, but light users wouldn't buy at all → lower output and welfare.
  • Tying also spreads R&D and fixed costs over more people.

Requiring products to be bought together as a package: Microsoft Office, cable TV, newspapers, Netflix, Amazon Prime.
Difference from tying: in tying goods are sold one-to-many (metered); in bundling goods are sold one-to-one.

Monopolistic Competition

From: 11. Monopolistic & oligopoly.pdf (slides 1–12)

7.1 Features

  • In between perfect competition and monopoly. It combines features of both.
  • Many buyers, many sellers, but each has some market power.
  • Differentiated products: close but not perfect substitutes. Unique or niche goods and services.
  • A firm that raises its price doesn't lose all its customers, so each firm has its own downward-sloping demand curve (a mini-monopoly that comes from consumer preferences).
  • Free entry and exit in the long run → zero economic profit in the long run (no supernormal profit).
  • Some economies of scale, but not enough for a natural monopoly.

It rests on three things: differentiation of products, consumer preferences/choice, and some degree of price discrimination. Examples: restaurants, salons, clothing brands, novels, movies, telecom data packs.

7.2 Short run vs long run

In the short run it behaves like a monopoly: MR = MC gives Q*, and price comes from its demand curve. It may earn a profit (P > ATC) or a loss (P < ATC).

  • Profits → new firms enter with similar products → each incumbent's demand curve shifts left (and gets flatter).
  • Losses → some firms exit → remaining firms' demand shifts right.
  • Long-run equilibrium: demand curve is tangent to ATC. P = ATC → zero economic profit, while still MR = MC and P > MC.

Oligopoly & Game Theory

From: 11. Monopolistic & oligopoly.pdf (slides 13–33) · Note on kinked demand curve.pdf

8.1 Features

  • Few sellers, many buyers → high market concentration. Relatively high entry barriers.
  • Sellers are interdependent: prices are "interactive". Each firm must guess how rivals will react.
  • Few sellers → incentive to cooperate (act like a monopoly). But self-interest → incentive to cheat.
  • Indian telecom example: DoCoMo, Aircel, Reliance CDMA, Airtel, BSNL, Jio, Vi.

Questions: What is the equilibrium? Is it closer to competition or monopoly? Is it socially desirable?

Competitive oligopoly
More firms, relatively lower entry barriers, homogeneous goods, saturated market. Prices are relatively close to ATC.
Collusive oligopoly
Few firms, high entry barriers, less competition. Collusion can be tacit (implicit understanding) or overt (open agreement, a cartel). Consumer loyalty and inertia on niche products.

8.2 Kinked demand curve: why oligopoly prices are sticky

The story: Airtel, Jio and Vi at ₹299
All three charge about ₹299 for a recharge. This is the current equilibrium.
  • Airtel raises price to ₹399: Jio probably does not follow. Customers shift to Jio and Airtel loses many. → Demand is very elastic above the current price.
  • Airtel cuts price to ₹199: Jio and Vi immediately match the cut. Airtel gains only a few customers. → Demand is relatively inelastic below the current price.
This change in slope at ₹299 is the kink.
  • Why MR becomes vertical: like a speed breaker on a road. Before it: smooth. After it: smooth. At it: a sudden jump. A sharp bend in demand creates a jump (discontinuity) in MR, which appears as the vertical segment.
  • Why it matters: MC can move up or down, but as long as MC cuts the vertical MR segment, the profit-maximising quantity (and price) doesn't change. Prices stay sticky.
  • Analogy: why don't Airtel, Jio and Vi change prices daily like vegetable vendors? Every price change triggers rival reactions; everyone fears a price war.
Summary chain
Kink in demand → Jump in MR → Vertical MR segment → Sticky prices.
Don't focus on the geometry, focus on the psychology: above the current price, rivals ignore you; below it, rivals attack you. That asymmetry creates the kink.

Market Failure

From: Market Failure renewed.pdf (final lecture) · Oligopoly deck slide 33

Why doesn't Adam Smith's "invisible hand" always give the best outcome? Markets fail when prices don't reflect all costs, benefits or information. Causes: asymmetric information (adverse selection, moral hazard), public goods, externalities. (Monopoly power, covered earlier, is another.)

1. Asymmetric information
One side knows more than the other → market unravels or exploitation.
Adverse selection (pre-contract), moral hazard (post-contract)
2. Public goods
Non-excludable & non-rival → under-provided.
Free-rider problem, tragedy of the commons
3. Externalities
Uncompensated effects on third parties.
Carbon pollution (−), education & vaccines (+)

9.1 Adverse selection: the quality collapse

Akerlof's "Market for Lemons"
Buyers can't judge true quality before buying (hidden characteristics), so they offer only an average price. High-quality sellers find this too low and exit, leaving only low-quality "lemons". Average quality falls → price falls further → more good sellers leave: a downward spiral.
Buyers can't tell quality→Offer average price→Good sellers exit→Average quality falls→Price falls again
  • Used EVs & battery degradation: buyers fear hidden battery decay and price all used EVs down; owners of pristine EVs hold back.
  • AI freelance platforms: clients can't tell skilled coders from AI-wrapper spammers, so pay falls for everyone.
  • Health insurance death spiral: sick people buy more cover, healthy people opt out → premiums rise for all → more healthy people leave.

9.2 Moral hazard: the principal–agent gap

Happens after the contract. The principal hires and delegates; the agent acts and holds private information. The agent's actions (effort) are unobservable and their incentives differ.

  • "Too big to fail" banks: bailout expectations encourage reckless risk-taking.
  • Cyber insurance carelessness: firms with full ransomware cover spend less on IT security.
  • Remote work: unobservable effort → shirking risk → performance-based contracts.

Solutions: deductibles and co-pays (insurance), performance contracts and equity grants, verified ratings and audits.

9.3 Signalling: how good players prove their quality

Hyundai's 10-year warranty
In the 1990s Hyundai was seen as low quality. It offered a costly 10-year / 100,000-mile warranty. A maker of bad cars would go bankrupt honouring it, so the warranty was a believable signal of quality.
Key rule: a signal must be costly to fake.

Modern examples: EV battery-health certificates (third-party telemetry), Airbnb verified identity and Superhost badges. Other fixes: screening, reputation registries, mandatory disclosure.

9.4 Public goods and common resources

Rival in consumptionNon-rival in consumption
ExcludablePrivate goods: smartphones, coffee, congested toll road, private apartments → markets work wellClub goods / natural monopoly: Netflix, Spotify, uncongested toll road, satellite internet → subscription pricing
Non-excludableCommon resources: ocean fish, congested free roads, groundwater → tragedy of the commons (over-use)Pure public goods: national defence, GPS, basic science research, clean air → free-rider collapse
  • Excludable: people who don't pay can be kept out. Rival: one person's use reduces what's left for others.
  • Free-rider problem: if you can't be excluded, why pay? Everyone waits for others to pay → private supply ≈ zero. Solution: government taxation and public provision.
  • Examples: Wikipedia and open-source software (used by billions, funded by under 2% of users), global disease surveillance, GPS and asteroid defence.
  • Tragedy of the commons: each user over-uses a shared resource because the cost falls on everyone (over-fishing, depleting groundwater).

9.5 Externalities

Negative externality → over-production
Private cost < social cost. Producers ignore harm to third parties and produce more than the social optimum.
Carbon emissions, antibiotic overuse → resistance, traffic congestion.
Positive externality → under-consumption
Private value < social value. People ignore spill-over benefits and consume less than optimal.
Vaccines and herd immunity, R&D spill-overs, higher education.

Left: the market produces Qm using private cost only. The socially efficient output is Qopt, where social cost meets demand. The red triangle is the DWL. A tax equal to the external cost moves the supply curve up to social cost and output back to Qopt. Right: with a positive externality the market under-produces; a subsidy fixes it.

9.6 Remedies: the policy toolkit

1. Pigouvian tax
Set tax = external cost, so private actors feel the social harm.
Carbon taxes, sugar taxes.
2. Tradable permits (cap & trade)
Set a pollution cap and let firms trade permits. Low-cost cleaners cut more and sell permits to high-cost polluters.
EU Emissions Trading System.
3. Ostrom commons
Elinor Ostrom (Nobel): local communities can manage shared resources without the state or privatisation.
Swiss Alpine pastures, irrigation systems.

Also: Coasean bargaining. If property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap, the affected parties can negotiate an efficient outcome privately.

Market failures in a nutshell
Information asymmetry → fix with credible signalling, screening, reputation, mandatory disclosure.
Public goods → need collective taxation and government funding (free-rider collapse).
Externalities → Pigouvian taxes, cap-and-trade, or Coasean property-rights bargaining.
Quick self-test
  1. Adverse selection vs moral hazard? → Hidden information before the contract vs hidden action after it.
  2. Why is GPS a public good? → Non-excludable and non-rival.
  3. Negative externality: is market output too high or too low? → Too high (Qm > Qopt).
  4. Why must a signal be costly? → Otherwise low-quality sellers could copy it too.

Classic traps

  • AFC is never U-shaped; it falls forever.
  • Diminishing MP ≠ falling output. Output falls only when MP < 0.
  • Diminishing returns (short run) ≠ diseconomies of scale (long run).
  • Zero economic profit ≠ zero accounting profit (normal profit).
  • Shutdown ≠ exit. A loss-making firm keeps producing in the SR as long as P ≥ AVC.
  • Monopolist reads price from the demand curve, not from MC or MR.
  • Perfect PD eliminates DWL but also eliminates consumer surplus.
  • Monopolistic competition: zero LR profit and P > MC (excess capacity).
  • Nash equilibrium isn't necessarily the best joint outcome (prisoner's dilemma).
