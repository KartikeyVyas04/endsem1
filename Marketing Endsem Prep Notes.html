Session 1Introduction & Core ConceptsDomain, Scope, Core Concepts

How marketing thinking evolvedSlides 2–7

The session opens with "What do you think is marketing?" and "Since when has marketing existed?" It then traces four concepts (orientations) in sequence:

ConceptWhat the slide saysPeriod (slide)
Production conceptConsumers prefer products that are widely available and inexpensive/highly affordable. The hallmarks of industrial production: mass production, scale economies, division of labour1850s – 1900s
Product conceptConsumers favour products that are superlative in quality, performance and new features. Managers focus entirely on quality and feature enhancement1900s – 1930s
Selling conceptSuccessor of the product era, at the peak of the Industrial Revolution. Kotler & Armstrong: companies can sell any product by persuading customers through advertising and personal selling1930s – 1950s
Marketing concept"Find the right products for customers rather than customers for products." Theodore Levitt's Marketing Myopia: define the business on the basis of customer needs1950s – early 21st century
Course book (BM.1, BM.3): The book adds examples and two more stages.
  • Production is useful in fast-growing industries, but firms "overly focused on cheap production can lose touch with the needs of the customer."
  • Product: Apple, whose fans wait for launches even when cheaper look-alikes exist. The risk is losing buyers who are driven by availability and price.
  • Selling: Coca-Cola keeps spending heavily on ads even though everyone knows the brand. Selling "doesn't usually lead to repeat purchases."
  • Marketing: the "customer-first approach", e.g., Glossier built skincare-friendly, easy make-up around unmet needs.
  • Societal concept (not on the slide): marketers have a moral responsibility to promote society's welfare. Fast food meets demand but harms health and the environment.
  • Digital era: data, personalisation and real-time feedback change the execution, not the principles.
  • The big shift is from inside-out (what we can produce and sell) to outside-in (what participants value).
  • Choosing an orientation (BM.3): firms rarely use a single philosophy. Production-driven firms measure efficiency, selling-driven firms measure conversion, and marketing-driven firms measure retention and satisfaction.
Note: the book dates the production concept to the 1950s. For exams, use the slide periods.

Marketing Myopia (Theodore Levitt)Slide 8

The growth of businesses and industries declines, slows or stops because of:

  • Management failure, not because the market was saturated
  • Failure to define the business from the viewpoint of customer needs
  • Inability to gauge obsolescence due to technological change

Businesspeople ought to understand that:

  • Industry is a customer-satisfying process, not a goods-producing process.
  • Industry begins with the customer and their needs.
  • Industry should start by delivering customer satisfaction and then work backwards to create the products.
Exam tip: When a firm in a case loses to a new kind of substitute (OTT vs cinema, UPI vs cards, EV vs petrol), diagnose myopia and redefine the business by the need it serves ("entertainment", "payments", "mobility"). This links to the threat of substitutes (S4–5) and bypass attacks (S18).

Defining Marketing and Marketing ManagementSlides 9–10

  • Marketing (AMA): "the activity, set of institutions, and processes for creating, communicating, delivering, and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners, and society at large."
  • Marketing management: "the art and science of choosing target markets and getting, keeping, and growing customers through creating, delivering, and communicating superior customer value."
  • In simple terms (slide 10): Create, communicate and deliver… customer value… profitably.
Course book (BM.4, BM.5):
  • Kotler: "the science and art of exploring, creating and delivering value to satisfy the needs of a target market at a profit." Marketing creates place, time, possession and awareness utilities.
  • Seven aspects of modern marketing: it is a societal process; it deals with needs, wants, products, pricing, distribution and promotion; it delivers value and satisfaction; it facilitates satisfying exchange relationships; it takes place in a dynamic environment; it is used by both for-profit and not-for-profit organisations; and it is important to business and the economy.
  • Selling vs marketing: selling focuses on short-term targets, on-the-spot persuasion, direct techniques (personal selling, cold calling, promotions) and quotas. Marketing focuses on long-term growth, brand awareness and trust, research and digital tools, and lasting relationships. Marketing generates leads and demand; selling converts leads. They align through communication, lead qualification and consistent messaging.
  • Marketing mix (Kotler): "the set of controllable variables the firm can use to influence the buyer's response": Product (a bundle of benefits: you buy comfort, not shoes), Price (the amount charged, set with demand, cost, ability to pay, competition and regulation in mind), Place (the distribution network), Promotion.

Customer ValueSlides 11–14, 17

Customer value / customer-perceived value (CPV) = the difference between the customer's evaluation of all the benefits and all the costs of an offering compared with perceived alternatives.

CPV = Total customer benefit − Total customer cost

Total customer benefitTotal customer cost
Product benefitMonetary cost
Services benefitTime cost
Personnel benefitEnergy cost
Image benefitPsychological cost

Slide 13 compares a Swiggy listing (Chai Coffi, Colaba) with a café photo: the same "coffee" delivers different benefits and costs depending on the setting. That difference is what CPV measures.

Customer value triad (slide 14): Quality · Service · Price. Customer satisfaction = the customer's judgment of product performance against expectations.

Course book (BM.6): Value = Total benefits − Total costs. Value is constructed in perception, not inherent in the product. Four types of value: functional (what it lets you do reliably), financial (savings, efficiency), social (recognition, belonging), psychological (confidence, identity, peace of mind). Value delivery sequence: Choose the value → Provide the value → Communicate the value. Communication must focus on the participant's priorities, not internal features.
Exam tip: CPV is the root logic of every case answer. You can raise value by adding benefits (product, service, personnel, image) or by cutting costs (money, time, energy, psychological). Name the lever your recommendation pulls.

Perception

Perception = the process by which we select, organise and interpret information inputs to create a meaningful picture of the world. Perceptual processes (slide 14): selective attention (including subliminal perception) and selective distortion. Slides 15–16 contrast a cinema hall with a theatre auditorium: similar spaces, but perceived differently. In marketing, perception matters more than reality.

Emotions

Mental states that arise spontaneously rather than through conscious effort. Brands develop an emotional connection through emotion-filled brand stories, and emotional states influence judgements and decisions.

Memory

The brain's ability to record, store and retrieve information. Memory model: nodes and links (associative network). Memory processes: encoding and retrieval. For example, brand associations are linked to the brand node and retrieved by certain stimuli. This is the basis of brand equity (S16).

Beyond the slides: selective retention (remembering only information that supports one's beliefs) is the third perceptual process. Learning works through drive → cue → response → reinforcement. Generalisation is the logic behind brand extensions; discrimination is the logic behind PODs. Self-concept can be actual, ideal or others' view. Lifestyle is measured by AIO (activities, interests, opinions).

Purchase Decision ProcessSlides 20–21

Problem recognition → Information search → Evaluation of alternatives → Purchase intention → Purchase decision
Course book (BM.7): the five stages, with a fifth stage for post-purchase.
  1. Need recognition: a gap between the current and desired state, triggered by internal stimuli (hunger) or external ones (ads, social influence). Example: an old, slow phone. Marketers use awareness campaigns.
  2. Information search: personal contacts, reviews, social media, websites. 87% of shoppers start online, so SEO and content matter.
  3. Evaluation of alternatives: comparing features, price, quality and reputation. Research reveals the criteria that matter (e.g., sustainability).
  4. Purchase decision: influenced by emotion, perceived value, promotions, availability, and friends and family. Make purchasing seamless (UX, payment options).
  5. Post-purchase behaviour: expectations vs reality lead to repeat purchase and loyalty, or returns and negative reviews. Use follow-ups, feedback and loyalty programmes.

Types of Buying BehaviourSlide 22

The slide plots involvement in the buying decision (low/high) against perceived difference between brands (low/high):

High difference between brandsLow difference between brands
High involvementComplex buyingInformation seeking (the textbook calls this dissonance-reducing)
Low involvementVariety seekingHabitual buying
Course book (BM.7): each type with its strategy.
  • Complex: expensive, infrequent, risky (a first car). The buyer forms beliefs → attitudes → a thoughtful choice. Strategy: knowledgeable staff; ads that shape beliefs and attitudes.
  • Dissonance-reducing: high involvement but few differences and limited choice (a camping table). Strategy: after-sales support that reassures the buyer about the choice.
  • Habitual: low involvement, routine items (an energy drink, chocolates). Bought from familiarity, with no real loyalty. Strategy: repetitive ads (radio, TV, print), visual symbols, price-drop and sales promotions for trial.
  • Variety-seeking: low involvement, significant differences, low switching cost (cookies). Switching comes from curiosity, not dissatisfaction. Strategy: the leader dominates shelf space, avoids stock-outs and advertises frequently; challengers use lower prices, deals, coupons and free samples.

Product / Brand Choice DecisionsSlide 23

Total set → Awareness set → Consideration set → Choice set → Choice (…other considerations)

The marketer's job is to get the brand from the total set into the consideration set and then into the choice set, and to know which rival brands sit in each set.

Beyond the slides: decision process detail (Kotler)
  • Information sources: personal (the most influential), commercial (the most exposure), public, experiential.
  • Evaluation: the expectancy-value model (attitude = Σ importance × belief) is compensatory. Non-compensatory shortcuts are conjunctive (a cut-off on every attribute), lexicographic (best on the most important attribute) and elimination-by-aspects.
  • Between intention and decision: the attitudes of others and unanticipated situational factors intervene.
  • Perceived risk: functional, physical, financial, social, psychological, time. Reduce it with guarantees, trials and brand trust.
  • Biases: anchoring (an MRP anchor), framing, mental accounting, the decoy effect.
  • Household buying roles: initiator, influencer, decider, buyer, user.
Course book (BM.7): emerging trends. (1) Digital and mobile: over 76% of shoppers use mobile to purchase; 54% of social users research products on social media, so a mobile-first approach and influencer marketing matter. (2) Sustainability and ethics: over 60% would pay a premium for sustainable packaging (McKinsey); fair labour and animal welfare matter; CSR. (3) Personalisation: data-driven insights and customised products.

Overarching Outcomes of an Environment ScanSlide 16

Identify fads, trends and megatrends · evaluate industry attractiveness · identify avenues for competitive advantage · develop a strategic response to each force · assess opportunities and threats, all leading to decisions.

Beyond the slides: a fad is short-lived with no lasting significance; a trend has momentum and durability; a megatrend is a large change lasting 7–10+ years (digitisation, urbanisation). Levels of competition: brand (Pepsi vs Coke) → industry (all soft drinks) → form (juices, water) → generic (all uses of the same money).

Measures of Market DemandSlides 23–25

Market demand = the total amount of a product that could be purchased (1) by a defined customer group, (2) in a defined geographic area, (3) in a defined time period, (4) under a defined marketing environment, (5) through a defined marketing programme.

Market potential = the maximum market demand, i.e., the highest demand reached as industry marketing expenditure approaches infinity.

Sessions 6–7Segmentation, Targeting & Positioning

Segmentation and Target MarketSlides 2–4

  • Segmentation = dividing a market into well-defined slices or groups of customers who share a similar set of needs and wants.
  • Target market = a set of buyers who share common needs and wants that a company decides to serve.

Why segmentation is important (slide 3): it is difficult to contact all customers in large, diverse markets; groups with distinct needs can be served more effectively; and it requires strategic planning and an understanding of consumer behaviour.

Segmentation criteria (slide 4): Differentiable (segments respond differently) · Measurable (size and purchasing power can be measured) · Substantial (large or profitable enough) · Accessible (can be reached and served) · Actionable (effective programmes can be designed for it).

PositioningSlides 14–18

  • Positioning = designing a company's offering and image to occupy a distinctive place in the minds of the target market.
  • A firm delivers specific, unique benefits through differentiated customer value (differentiation) and positioning. Together they provide competitive advantage, which should be crafted so it cannot easily be copied.
  • Sustainable competitive advantage comes from (1) differentiating an existing attribute, (2) introducing a new attribute, or (3) building a strong brand.
Positioning step (slides 16–17)What it involves
1. Determine the competitive frame of referenceSet the frame of brands that compete with ours; establish category membership; identify competitors
2. Identify points of parity and points of differencePOPs = attributes or benefits the brand shares with others in the frame. PODs = attributes unique to the brand that consumers strongly associate with it. PODs must be desirable, deliverable, differentiable
3. Craft the positioning statement / brand mantraIndicates the brand essence or brand promise. A 3–5-word phrase capturing the brand's "heart and soul". Criteria: clarity of communication, simplicity, inspiration

Slide 18 examples: Snickers (a chocolate bar positioned against hunger: "You're not you when you're hungry"), Mahindra Scorpio (a rugged, powerful SUV), Appy Fizz (a sparkling apple drink framed as a cool adult party drink, not a kids' juice).

Course book (STP): Factors that affect positioning: a larger target market makes positioning harder; with no competition the firm can create a completely new position; existing brand value helps position new products; lower prices than rivals give an advantage. Starbucks positioned itself as the "third place" between home and work, for medium-to-high-income customers. McDonald's full STP: segments by demographics (Happy Meals for kids, value meals), psychographics (healthy options, late-night hours) and behaviour (regulars vs occasional visitors); targets families, young adults, teens and children; positions on convenience, affordability, variety, consistency. Bank example: segment by demographics and behaviour → target young professionals new to investing → position as a friendly, educational resource. Benefits of STP: sharper customer focus, marketing efficiency, engagement, distinct brand positioning, data-driven decisions.
Beyond the slides: POP types: category, correlational, competitive. Differentiation bases: product, services, personnel, channel, image. Value propositions: more for more, more for the same, same for less, less for much less, more for less. Positioning errors: under-, over-, confused and doubtful positioning. A perceptual map plots brands on two attributes to reveal gaps.
Exam tip: Start every case recommendation with STP: who exactly is the target, and what is the POD for them? A 4P plan without a clear segment and position is the most common weak answer.

Basics of PriceSlide 2

  • Price is the only element of the 4Ps that generates revenue; all the others lead to costs.
  • Price shows up in many forms: wage, rent, commission, toll, tariff, tuition, fare, etc.
  • Pricing decisions are complex and must take into account the company (costs), customers (their assessment of price), competition (prices), and the marketing environment.
Course book (BM.8): Pricing = determining the right value a customer pays in exchange for a product. It balances profitability, satisfaction and competitiveness, and it considers production cost, demand, competitor pricing and perceived value.
Objectives of pricing (book)Meaning
Revenue generationMaximise total revenue by balancing price and volume
Market leadershipDominate through competitive, affordable-yet-profitable prices; builds recognition and loyalty
SurvivalIn unstable or competitive markets (and downturns), cover costs and keep cash flowing
Profit maximisationOptimise the cost–price–demand relationship
Customer attraction and retentionFair prices acquire and retain customers, giving long-term revenue

Importance of pricing (book): (1) it creates the first impression; (2) it ensures the right price level (wrong prices can even close a business); (3) it promotes sales growth (discounts clear inventory); (4) it gives flexibility (the most flexible P); (5) it drives profit; (6) it builds a competitive edge (value-based pricing stresses uniqueness); (7) it manages demand (off-season discounts, peak prices).

Setting the Price: 6 StepsSlides 5–7

1. Select the pricing objective → 2. Determine demand at different prices → 3. Estimate costs → 4. Analyse competitors' costs, prices and offers → 5. Select a pricing method → 6. Select the final price

Step 1: Select the pricing objective (slide 6)

ObjectiveMeaning / when it works
Short-term profit / maximise current profitChoose the price that maximises current profit and cash flow. It ignores long-run effects and competitor reactions
Market penetration (maximise market share)A low price to build volume and share. Works when the market is highly price-sensitive, costs fall with volume, and a low price discourages competitors
Market skimmingA high launch price, reduced over time to "skim" successive layers. Works when there are enough buyers with high demand, small-volume costs aren't too high, the high price won't attract rivals, and it signals a superior product
Quality leadershipPremium quality at a premium but reachable price ("affordable luxury": Starbucks, Apple)

Step 2: Determine demand: price elasticity (slide 6)

Price elasticity of demand = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

Elastic (|E| > 1): price cuts raise revenue. Inelastic (|E| < 1): price rises raise revenue. Demand is less elastic when there are few substitutes, buyers don't notice the price, habits are slow to change, higher prices seem justified, or the price is a small share of total cost.

Step 3: Estimate costs (slide 6)

  • Fixed costs (don't vary with output: rent, salaries), variable costs (vary with output: materials), total costs = FC + VC.
  • Experience curve: average cost falls as accumulated production experience grows. Experience-curve pricing means pricing low to build volume and move down the curve faster than rivals. Risks: a cheap image, rivals leapfrogging to lower-cost technology, price wars.

Step 4: Analyse competitors' prices (slide 7)

Understand competitors' costs and prices (add value for your superior features, subtract for inferior ones) and assess their possible reactions.

Step 5: Select a pricing method (slide 7)

MethodHow it works
Mark-up pricingAdd a standard mark-up to cost. Unit cost = VC + FC ÷ unit sales; Mark-up price = Unit cost ÷ (1 − desired return on sales). Simple, but ignores demand and competition
Target-rate-of-return pricingPrice = Unit cost + (Desired ROI × Invested capital) ÷ Unit sales. Check the break-even volume: BEV = FC ÷ (Price − VC)
Economic-Value-to-Customer (EVC) pricingPrice based on the value the customer perceives relative to the next-best alternative (performance, service, brand), not on cost
Competitive / going-rate pricingPrice largely based on competitors' prices (same, higher or lower). Common in commodities and oligopolies
Auction pricingEnglish (ascending bids), Dutch (descending: starts high and falls; or one buyer and many sellers), sealed-bid (tenders)
Worked example: VC = ₹10, FC = ₹3,00,000, expected sales 50,000 → unit cost = 10 + 6 = ₹16. A 20% mark-up on sales gives 16 ÷ 0.8 = ₹20. Target 20% ROI on ₹10 lakh → 16 + (0.2 × 10,00,000) ÷ 50,000 = ₹20. Break-even at ₹20 = 3,00,000 ÷ (20 − 10) = 30,000 units.
Course book (BM.8), classic pricing methods: (1) Cost-based: sale price = cost price + % profit. Simple, but it may ignore perceived value and market conditions. (2) Competition-based ("competency-based" in the book): adjust to competitors' prices. Ideal in saturated markets, but risky if perceived value is ignored. (3) Demand-based: prices rise when demand is high and fall when it is low.

Initiating and Responding to Price ChangesSlide 9

Initiating price cuts (slide)

  • Clearing excess inventory or capacity
  • Other marketing strategies have little effect on sales

Traps: the low-quality trap (cheap looks poor), the fragile-market-share trap (buys share, not loyalty), the shallow-pockets trap (richer rivals outlast you), and the price-war trap.

Initiating price increases (slide)

  • Managing inflation (cost increases)
  • Rise in demand (over-demand)
  • Product improvement

Ways to raise prices: delayed quotation pricing, escalator clauses, unbundling, reducing discounts. Alternatives: a smaller pack (shrinkflation), cheaper materials, dropping features.

Responding to competitors' price changes (slide)

Options: maintain the price (and margin) · maintain the price and add value · reduce the price · raise the price and improve quality · launch a low-price fighter line. Consider: the product's PLC stage, its importance in the portfolio, the competitor's intent and resources, the market's price and value sensitivity, and whether the move is temporary or permanent.

Exam tip: When a strong brand faces a cheap attacker, don't cut the flagship's price (the traps above, plus lost image-pricing value). Better: add value, raise quality, or launch a fighter brand (the flank defence in S18). Cut price only if demand is elastic and you have the cost advantage.

Distribution ChannelsSlide 2

  • Distribution (marketing) channels are "sets of interdependent organisations participating in the process of making a product or service available for use or consumption."
  • They move products from producers to consumers (delivering value).
  • They help overcome the gaps of time, place and possession.
  • Distribution involves a set of intermediaries: merchants (buy, take title and resell: wholesalers, retailers), agents (search for customers and negotiate but don't take title: brokers), and facilitators (assist without taking title or negotiating: transporters, warehouses, banks).
Course book (BM.10): A distribution channel is the network that moves goods from producers to end consumers: wholesalers, retailers, distributors and digital platforms.
  • Direct channel: the manufacturer sells directly to the consumer. It is shorter and can mean lower costs for consumers.
  • Indirect channel: sold through a wholesaler or retailer (typical of brick-and-mortar goods).
  • Hybrid channel: both direct and indirect.
  • Digital technology lets even small firms reach consumers directly through e-commerce.
  • Choosing a channel: consider consumer preferences, speed to market, the value intermediaries add, and the potential for channel conflict.

  • 10 components: advertising · PR (media relations, sponsorships, influencers) · direct marketing (email, SMS, telemarketing) · sales promotion · virtual/digital marketing (SEO, PPC, social) · content marketing (blogs, videos, podcasts) · social media marketing · partnerships and sponsorships · internal communication (employees as brand ambassadors) · digital tools (CRM, analytics, automation).
  • Examples: Coca-Cola "Share a Coke" (names on bottles + #ShareACoke + PR + experiential); Nike "Just Do It" (print, social, athletes, events); Apple launch events (teasers, media invites, live streams); Red Bull content marketing (Red Bull Media House, extreme sports, festivals); Dove "Real Beauty" (ads, social, PR, user-generated content).
IMC planning: Analyse the problem → Set goals → Identify the audience → Develop key messages → Choose channels → Develop strategy → Execute → Monitor results → Revise

Step 5: Develop the media plan (slide 13)

  • Weigh audience size, composition and media cost, measured as cost per thousand persons reached (CPM): CPM = Media cost × 1,000 ÷ Audience reached.
  • Also weigh audience quality, audience attention, editorial quality, ad-placement policies and extra services.
  • Determine reach (R: % of the target exposed at least once), frequency (F: average number of exposures) and impact (I: the qualitative value of an exposure in a given medium).

Exposure (Gross Rating Points, GRP) = R × F · Weighted exposure = R × F × I

Example: reach 60%, frequency 3 → GRP = 180. With impact 1.5, weighted exposure = 270. A launch needs reach; a complex message, low loyalty or frequent purchase needs frequency. A fixed budget forces a trade-off between the two.

Step 6: Measure the performance (slide 14)

Supply sideDemand side
Assessment of media coverage: the number of seconds the ad is visible, column inches of a print adEffect on the target audience: recall of the ad, change in attitude toward the brand, change in purchase intent
Common trap: The slides use 6 steps, while Kotler's textbook uses 8 (audience → objectives → design → channels → budget → mix → measure → manage IMC). In the exam, use the slide sequence and note that the budget sits inside step 1.
Beyond the slides: communication concepts from Kotler
  • Rossiter–Percy objectives: category need, brand awareness (recognition vs recall), brand attitude, purchase intention. DAGMAR: set specific, measurable communication goals.
  • Message structure: one-sided arguments suit favourable audiences; two-sided arguments suit educated or opposed audiences. Fear appeals work at moderate levels when a solution is offered. Celebrity risks: overshadowing the brand, overexposure, scandal.
  • Media timing: continuity, concentration, flighting (bursts and gaps), pulsing (a low base plus bursts).
  • Paid, owned and earned media; buzz and viral marketing; micro-influencers. PR functions: press relations, product publicity, corporate communication, lobbying, counselling.
  • Sales force: structure (territorial, product, market, complex), size (workload approach), compensation (fixed, variable, expenses, benefits). Selling steps: prospecting → pre-approach → presentation → objections → close → follow-up.
  • Measurement: copy testing (pre/post), sales-effect research, share of voice, digital metrics (CTR, CPC, CPA, ROAS).

Part B — Brand Management: What is a Brand?Slides 16–18

Brand (AMA): "a name, term, sign, symbol, or design, or a combination of them, intended to identify the goods or services of one seller or group of sellers and to differentiate them from those of competitors." Branding is the process of endowing products and services with the power of a brand.

Slide 18 logos: Asian Paints, Titan, SBI (corporate names used as brands) and Unilever → Lux (a corporate parent behind a separately named product brand). These preview the brand architecture choices on slide 25.

Course book (BM.9): A brand is the idea or image people hold about a company's products, both practical ("the shoe is lightweight") and emotional ("it makes me feel powerful"). It is triggered by the name, logo, visual identity or message. A product can be copied, but a brand is unique: Pepsi and Coca-Cola taste similar, yet people connect with one or the other. Water brands give the same product different meanings: Evian makes you feel young, Perrier is refreshing, bubbly and sexy, and Fiji is pure, healthy and natural. A product is what you sell, a brand is the perceived image of what you sell, and branding is the strategy to create that image.

Brand EquitySlide 20

Case 1 — Premium café chain vs a ₹99-coffee attacker (S11, S18, S6–7)
BrewNest (a premium café with a ₹250 average ticket) is losing young customers to a ₹99-coffee kiosk chain. The board wants a 40% price cut.

Diagnose: the price-sensitive youth segment is defecting; experience-seekers are not. Don't cut the flagship's price: it triggers the low-quality, shallow-pockets and price-war traps and destroys image pricing (S11).

Options (S11 responses): maintain price and add value (loyalty programme, work-friendly spaces); a fighter sub-brand ("BrewNest Go" kiosks at ₹120), which is a flank defence (S18); third-degree pricing (student off-peak offers).

Recommend: add value on the flagship plus the fighter sub-brand. Keep the two positions separate (S6–7), and use selective kiosks near colleges (S13). Measure: flagship margin, sub-brand cannibalisation, repeat visits.

Case 2 — FMCG brand goes D2C; distributors revolt (S13)
A snack brand's website sells 15% cheaper. Distributors and kiranas have cut its shelf space and are pushing a rival.

Diagnose: multichannel and vertical conflict, caused by goal incompatibility and unclear rights.

Fix (slide tools): price parity online; strategic justification (online-only combos and subscriptions for a different segment); dual compensation (credit distributors for online orders in their territory); superordinate goal (beat the rival brand); co-optation (a distributor advisory council). Use reward and expert power, not coercive power. Add trade incentives (display allowances, free goods: S11) to win back shelf space. This matters because intensive FMCG distribution depends on the trade.

Case 3 — Launching a patented AI hearing aid (S11, S13, S14, S18)
A startup has a far superior, patent-protected hearing aid. Advise on price and go-to-market.

Situate: PLC introduction; complex buying; patents block rivals.

Price: skimming (all four slide conditions hold) using EVC against the next-best alternative, with a guarantee and a trial period to cut perceived risk. Place: selective distribution through audiologists and ENT clinics (service backup). Promotion: informational appeal, expert source (doctors), PR, demonstrations; objective = awareness → preference. Budget: objective-task.

Case 4 — Hotel chain with inconsistent service (S16)
A mid-range chain advertises "5-star comfort at 3-star prices." Reviews cite slow check-in, rude staff, and rooms unlike the photos.

Gaps: the over-promising ad = gap 4; slow check-in and rude staff = gap 3; no check-in standard = gap 2. Variability fixes: training, standardised processes, satisfaction monitoring. RATER: reliability (rooms as shown), responsiveness (check-in), assurance and empathy (staff), tangibles. Best practices: high standards, monitoring systems, handling every complaint, satisfying employees to satisfy customers. Reposition honestly, and measure review ratings and repeat stays.

Case 5 — Premium watch brand wants a ₹2,000 range (S10, S16)
A premium Indian watch brand (₹15,000+) wants to enter the ₹2,000 youth segment.

Concepts: down-market stretch (growth, but a risk of dilution and cannibalisation) and brand-extension fit (S16).

Recommend: a sub-brand or separate brand with light parent endorsement (the Titan → Fastrack logic). Give it its own youth imagery and distinct distribution, and keep the parent's premium position intact. Mix: penetration-leaning price, transformational appeals, social media.

Case 6 — Regional detergent challenging the national leader (S18, S2–3)
A regional detergent brand strong in two states, with a limited budget, wants to grow against the national leader.

Avoid a frontal attack. Use a flank attack (underserved small towns and villages; a hard-water formula; ₹5 packs) and guerrilla tactics (local promotions, retailer schemes).

Porter: cost focus. Ansoff: market development into neighbouring states. Buyers are habitual or variety-seeking, so strong push and trade incentives, shelf presence and sampling. Measure: district share, numeric distribution, trial and repeat rates.

Case 7 — Legacy biscuit brand losing relevance (S16, S10, S14)
A 60-year-old biscuit brand has near-universal awareness, but young consumers see it as old-fashioned.

Diagnose: high salience, weak imagery and feelings (resonance pyramid). This is a revitalisation problem, not an awareness problem.

Actions: new usage occasions; line modernising and a modern pack (S10); transformational appeals; co-branding with a youth brand. Keep the core POPs (taste, trust). Test the creative with ADPLAN, especially linkage. Measure through brand tracking of youth imagery.

Case 8 — Pricing numerical (S11)
VC = ₹40, FC = ₹6,00,000, expected sales 20,000 units, investment ₹20 lakh. Find (a) the mark-up price at a 25% return on sales, (b) the target-return price at 15% ROI, and (c) the BEV at (b).

Unit cost = 40 + 30 = ₹70. (a) 70 ÷ 0.75 = ₹93.33. (b) 70 + (0.15 × 20,00,000) ÷ 20,000 = ₹85. (c) 6,00,000 ÷ (85 − 40) = 13,333 units.

Elasticity check: if a 10% price cut raises volume by 25%, E = −2.5 (elastic), so the cut increases revenue.

Case 9 — Shampoo brand going rural (S6–7, S13, S18, S1)
An urban shampoo brand (₹180 bottles) has 3% rural penetration and wants rural growth without hurting its urban image.

Ansoff: market development. CPV: cut the monetary cost per purchase with ₹2–₹5 sachets. Segmentation: geographic (rural) plus ISEC classes. Distribution: intensive rural reach (hub-and-spoke, haats, women-entrepreneur networks). Promotion: sampling for trial, and regional-language, low-literacy media. Keep the pack formats and messages distinct so the urban positioning is protected (strategic justification).

Case 10 — B2B software firm losing tenders (S2–3, S14)
A mid-sized ERP firm with strong features keeps losing large tenders. Buyers say, "We weren't sure you could support us."

Diagnose: a new buy with high risk. The buying centre values credibility and support over features. Actions: map the roles (users: ease of use; influencers: technical fit; deciders and approvers: ROI and risk; gatekeepers: procurement); provide reference clients; offer pilots and service guarantees; build a trust, credibility and reputation strategy (slide 30); use personal and consultative selling and PR. Measure: shortlist rate, win rate.

Case 11 — Multiplex with empty weekday seats (S16, S11)
A multiplex runs at 85% weekend occupancy but 20% on weekday afternoons, with uniform prices.

Perishability. Demand side: differential pricing (₹99 weekday matinées, which is third-degree, time-based pricing); boost non-peak demand (student and senior shows, corporate bookings, live-sports screenings); complementary services (food and drink combos, i.e., bundling); reservations and subscription passes (two-part pricing). Supply side: part-time staff at weekends. Measure: weekday occupancy, revenue per seat.

Case 12 — Media-plan numerical (S14)
Budget ₹2 crore. Option A: TV (reach 70%, frequency 2, cost ₹1.8 cr, impact 1.2). Option B: digital + outdoor (reach 45%, frequency 5, cost ₹1.5 cr, impact 1.0). The product is a new energy drink for youth.

GRP: A = 140, B = 225. Weighted exposure: A = 168, B = 225. Cost per weighted GRP: A ≈ ₹1.07 lakh, B ≈ ₹0.67 lakh. B is more efficient, reaches the youth target, and leaves ₹50 lakh for sampling (trial). A would be better only if the goal were broad mass reach. Measure ad recall and trial rate.
