01Primer to Research — Introduction to Research Thinking

Session 1. The philosophical foundation: what research is, what science is, why science beats the alternatives, and the five traits that make a method "scientific".

1.1 What does "research" actually mean?

The word breaks into "Re" (again) + "Search" (find) — literally searching again and again. That etymology carries the whole idea: research is a continuous process, and nothing is ever final.

Definition — the four framings you should be able to reproduce
  1. Booth et al. (2016), The Craft of Research: Research is a "never-ending conversation" which helps us (the researcher community) free all of us (the general public) from ignorance, prejudice, and the half-baked ideas that so many charlatans try to impose on us.
  2. Western Sydney University (2023): Research is the creation of new knowledge and/or the use of existing knowledge in a new and creative way so as to generate new concepts, methodologies and understandings — including synthesis and analysis of previous research, to the extent it leads to new and creative outcomes.
  3. Dictionary / definitional aspect: a careful, systematic investigation of a subject to establish facts or principles, or to discover new information (Oxford, Collins, Cambridge, Britannica, Merriam-Webster, American Heritage all converge on this).
  4. Bhattacherjee (2012) — the two-condition test. See below; this is the one most likely to be examined.
Exam focus — Bhattacherjee's two conditions

For work to count as research, BOTH must hold:

  1. It must add to the body of knowledge.
  2. The process must be scientific.

If only one holds, it is not research:

  • Only (i) — adds knowledge, no scientific method: the '5+2' observation in the Corona death case (Nathan & Posina, 2020) — a pattern was noticed in Worldometer COVID data and it told us something, but no systematic method produced it.
  • Only (ii) — scientific process, adds nothing new: a perfectly executed study that merely re-confirms something already firmly established, or a methodologically flawless survey with no new insight.

A very common exam framing: "Is X research? Justify." → Apply the two conditions explicitly.

Why are PhD theses never graded?

Because a thesis is judged on whether it makes an original contribution to knowledge — a binary "does it advance the conversation or not" — not on a scale. Knowledge is not a marks-out-of-100 thing; the "never-ending conversation" either gains something or it doesn't.

1.2 What is Science? — three aspects

The course approaches "science" from three angles. Learn the three-aspect structure — it is the cleanest way to answer "What is science?" in an exam.

AspectCore idea
ConceptualScience is about how we know what we know — validating ideas — not merely what we know.
DisciplinaryScience has no disciplinary boundary; it covers natural, social and formal sciences.
Comparative
(ways of knowing)		Science is one of four ways of fixing belief, and the only self-testing one.

(a) Conceptual aspect

From Latin scientia = 'knowledge' of a fact or situation, or 'understanding' of a subject — as opposed to 'mere belief' (Oxford Latin Dictionary, 1968).

The two examples used in class
  • If we merely believe that obese children are more likely to become obese adults than their thinner peers — that is not science.
  • If we merely believe that women-headed households are more likely to adopt modern cooking fuels than male-headed ones — that is not science.

It becomes science only when we encounter scientific evidence for these claims, or arrive at them ourselves through systematic study.

  • Science is essentially a method, not so much its outcome.
  • Its outcomes are not definitive — they are tentative. Karl Popper (The Logic of Scientific Discovery, 2002): there is a perpetual necessity for every scientific statement to remain tentative forever.
  • In the realm of science there is no absolute book, no absolute person, no absolute authority.
Quotable — Clark et al. (2017, pp.16–17)

"Science isn't about certainty, it isn't merely about the orderly collection of facts, and it isn't about invoking authority to protect our ideas from uncomfortable evidence. Instead science is about asking tough questions and providing answers that invite criticism. Science is about recognizing the limits of our knowledge without lapsing into irresponsible cynicism. And science is about using the best logic, methods, and evidence available to provide answers today, even though we recognize that they may be overturned tomorrow."

→ Science therefore invokes strong morality, humility, responsibility, courage, and commitment to truth.

(b) Disciplinary aspect

Conventionally, dictionaries define science as knowledge of the physical/natural world, provable by experiment. This is a limitation — the words 'human', 'society' and 'social' are missing from them (Heintz, 2009). They reinforce that science = knowledge, and they emphasise the 'how', but they exclude the social.

The Science Council's corrective definition memorise

"Science is the pursuit and application of knowledge and understanding of the natural and social world following a systematic methodology based on evidence."

BranchExamplesNote
Natural sciencesPhysics, chemistry, biology, geologyThe boundary blurs — e.g. epidemiology sits across both.
Social sciencesEconomics, psychology, sociology
Formal sciencesLogic, mathematics, statistics, theoretical linguisticsFormal sciences are the "grammar" of natural and social science — the syntax of symbols used to convey ideas; tools/rules running through both.

Hansson (2013, p.64): science in the broad sense seeks knowledge about nature (natural science), about ourselves (psychology, medicine), about our societies (social science, history), about our physical constructions (technological science), and about our thought constructions (linguistics, literary studies, mathematics, philosophy).

The line worth quoting

"Science is not partial to any discipline. Rather, disciplines are partial to science. Disciplines which use science less may benefit by doing it more."

1.3 The hierarchy of science, and where social science sits

Comte (19th century) arranged the sciences in a hierarchy. The diagram used in class maps the scale of the universe (from particle → atom → cell → human → Earth → solar system → galaxy → visible universe) onto branches of science:

Formal (logic, maths)→Physical (physics, chemistry)→Life (cellular & functional biology)→Social (psychology, sociology, economics)→Earth & Space

Logic and mathematics sit at the base as the most general/abstract; sociology, law, ethics and economics sit at the "human scale". The point of the diagram: the social sciences are not outside the hierarchy of science.

What social science does

  • Analysing and studying the phenomena of society — inequality? social chaos?
  • Predicting the behaviour of people
  • Inferring decisions from social phenomena

Note how closely this mirrors a rural manager's role — which is exactly why this course exists.

Why studying society is hard
  • We have less definitive tools that can predict society.
  • Our understanding of society is ever evolving.
  • It involves complex relationships between people — and are humans really rational?
  • "It is like doing a very unpredictable lab experiment."

This is precisely why qualitative methods are needed — instruments that capture meaning, context and subjectivity rather than only numbers.

1.4 Is science the only way of knowing? — Peirce's four methods

Charles Peirce, The Fixation of Belief (1877) (as discussed via Johnstone, 1962; Ray, 2021). Practically, there are four ways of fixing belief. This is an almost guaranteed exam question — know all four with examples.

1.5 Characteristics of the scientific method of research high yield

Five characteristics. Expect "Explain the characteristics of the scientific method" as a 10-marker.

(1) Replicability

Others should be able to repeat the study and get the same/similar result under the same environment (Freese & Peterson, 2017). This is straightforward for quantitative social science.

Replication in QUALITATIVE social science — why it is problematic

Four reasons (Freese & Peterson, 2017):

  1. Anonymity — participants are promised confidentiality, so raw data often cannot be shared for re-analysis.
  2. Evolution during research — the research enquiries and questions themselves evolve during the study.
  3. Interpretative enterprise — analysis depends on interpretation, which is researcher-specific.
  4. Interviewer is part of the observation — the interview is part of a larger setting and of the interviewer's own experience.

Hence the line: "Field notes lose much of their value when divorced from the note taker."

(2) Precision

Concepts must be defined with precision (Bhattacherjee, 2012). Precision is needed while:

  • defining variables;
  • choosing data collection instruments/methods;
  • choosing the measurement scale (if any);
  • concluding what is found — e.g. not confusing correlation with causation (Kazdin, 2016).

Examples of hard-to-define concepts: measurement of learning abilities; quality of education imparted; quality of students.

(3) Falsifiability

Attributed to Karl Popper. A theory or hypothesis must be stated in such a way that it can be disproved if new data or information comes along (Bhattacherjee, 2012). It is the difference between science and non-science, and requires a testable hypothesis (Kazdin, 2016).

Example

"A higher spending in health contributes to greater human capital formation." — this is falsifiable: data could show health spending rising while human capital indicators stay flat. A claim that can never be contradicted by any possible evidence is not scientific.

(4) Parsimony

Attributed to Ockham (Occam's razor). Parsimony examines how much of a phenomenon is explained with how few variables. When multiple explanations exist for the same phenomenon, the simplest explanation that adequately fits the evidence is usually preferred.

  • It does not mean the simplest explanation is always true — only that it is generally the best starting point.
  • A more complex explanation takes precedence only if the simplest one becomes inadequate.
  • If there are competing, equally complex explanations — all will remain.
Example

How can we improve children's health? Start the enquiry with food intake (a direct link) rather than arguing about trade deals on infant foods (distant/indirect).

Mnemonic for the characteristics

R-P-F-P → Replicability, Precision, Falsifiability, Parsimony. ("Real Professors Find Problems")

2.1 Purpose and motivations of research

Definitions

  • Srivastava & Rego (2011) — in management: research "guides managers to make informed decisions to deal with problems, collect facts/observations, and present them in a systematic/logical manner."
  • Research is an attempt towards solving problems — not all problems get solved by research.
  • If you are in the research mould, it is more likely you will find something new — including the knowledge that we don't know something.

2.2 How to find a research problem

What is a "problem"? (O'Leary, 2005)

A problem is a situation where there is a gap between what is real and what is ideal or desired.

But what counts as "ideal" is not always straightforward — there is an "element of subjectivity", and it evolves with cultural shifts.

Examples: the state of the environment (the shift from MDGs to SDGs changed what "ideal" means); gender wage difference (once unremarkable, now a problem).

Problem vs. research problem: a research problem must bridge the gap — it must make a difference. And selecting one is debatable, because of the practicality aspect: political and financial realities.

Five sources for identifying gaps

SourceExample given in class
Real-life issuesPolitical leaders switching parties post-election.
Own experienceLate diagnosis of diseases and survival rate.
Literature reviewPapers state their limitations and give the future scope of research — e.g. the GDI measure in human development.
Stakeholder consultationAssessing the views of those who have a stake — note "your problem" vs "my problem" (Nathan, 2018).
Media coverage / newspapersEmerging issues surfacing in public discourse.

2.3 Unpacking a research problem

O'Leary's three-circle model of an 'unpacked' problem

An 'unpacked' problem sits at the intersection of three explorations:

  1. Exploring dominant worldviews — what does the mainstream/received wisdom assume?
  2. Exploring stakeholder perspectives — how do those affected see it?
  3. Exploring personal perspectives — what are my assumptions and biases?

Draw this as three overlapping circles with 'Unpacked Problems' in the centre — it is an easy diagram mark.

Problem diagnosing

Problems are generally complex, multi-faceted and inconsistent in nature — look closely.

Worked example — "Why higher mortality from non-communicable diseases (say cancer)?"
  • Is there no treatment facility?
  • Is access to treatment inadequate?
  • Is it late diagnosis because of poor awareness?

Each sub-diagnosis points to a completely different study.

Also explore the assumptions embedded in the problem definition:

  • "What kind of health-seeking behaviour do people have?" — assumes accessibility.
  • "Utilization of public health care facilities" — assumes availability.

Practicality while choosing a research problem

  • Is it feasible for you to do as a small-scale research study?
  • Limited budget? (e.g. the threat of a major asteroid strike is a real problem for the human race — but not one you can research.)
  • Support and funding are enabling conditions.
  • Unpopular research is welcome — but you may have to garner support for it.

2.4 From problem to research question

Why narrow the problem down? To make it clearer; to define investigations and boundaries; to give direction; to benchmark your decision making (O'Leary, 2005). The research question is "the door to a research study" (Flick, 2014).

Easterly (2008) on the MDGs

Problem: Africa is not doing well on the MDGs.
One possible research question: "Were the MDGs designed to be unfair to Africa?"
Key lesson: the same problem can lead to entirely different research questions.

Narrowing funnel — worked example

What am I interested in? → Women's issues? Health of a society? Rural entrepreneurship?
Say health of society. What particularly?

  • Why are non-communicable diseases now more prevalent?
  • What kind of health-seeking behaviour do people have?
  • What is the rate of utilization of public health care facilities?
  • How does health expenditure impact overall household welfare?

2.5 From question to hypothesis

Definition (O'Leary, 2005)

A hypothesis is a logical conjecture — a hunch or an educated guess: the nature of relationships between two or more variables, expressed as a testable statement.

Problem→Research question→Hypothesis (your hunch)→Variables→Relationship to be tested
The recycling example, all the way through
  • Question: "Is there a relationship between household recycling behaviours and demographic characteristics?"
  • Hunch: recycling behaviour ~ age of household members.
  • Hypothesis: "Children and teenagers are more likely than adults to put inappropriate materials in recycle bins."
  • Variables: recycling behaviour and age. Relationship tested: recycling behaviour depends on age.
When there is NO scope for a hypothesis

"How do individuals engage in decision-making processes related to household domestic waste management?" — this is an exploratory study. You are exploring and uncovering a process, so no hypothesis is needed.

Another example: "What are the reasons behind the high incidence of water-borne diseases in this area?"

This happens more often in qualitative research. Course refrain: "No hypothesis — no problem."

In-class activity (know the drill — it can be set as an exam scenario)

Scenario: A district in rural Gujarat has seen a sharp rise in diabetes and hypertension over the last decade. Despite this, most Primary Health Centres report very few patients coming for regular check-ups or early screening. Local health workers say people mostly show up only after symptoms become severe.

The four-step drill:

  1. Diagnose the broad problem (gap between real and ideal; unpack assumptions — availability? awareness? affordability? trust? gendered mobility?)
  2. Generate research questions by the nature of your enquiry — same problem, different questions (descriptive / exploratory / explanatory / action-oriented).
  3. Test with the six-point checklist.
  4. Develop a hypothesis — or state explicitly why this is exploratory and needs none.

3.3 Research approaches: inductive and deductive high yield

Definitions

Deduction is the process of drawing conclusions about a phenomenon or behaviour based on theoretical or logical reasons and an initial set of premises. General → specific (top-down).

Induction is the process of drawing conclusions based on facts or observed evidence. Induction occurs when we observe a fact and ask, "Why is this happening?" Specific → general (bottom-up).

InductiveDeductive
PurposeTo seek insights into an observed phenomenonTo test or validate a theory
OutcomeTheory buildingTheory testing
DirectionSpecific → general (bottom-up); depthGeneral → specific (top-down); breadth
Typical strategyExploratory — associated with qualitative researchConfirmatory — associated with quantitative research
Examples in classRelationship between heights of parents and children (Galton's observation of regression to the mean — you observe the data and then ask why)Flat-earth theory; theory of demand; Prospect Theory (you start with the theory and go test it)
The heights example, explained

Plot fathers' heights against sons' heights. The average height of sons of tall fathers is lower than their fathers' average; the average height of sons of short fathers is higher than their fathers' average. Both drift back toward the overall average. You observed this from data first, then asked "why is this happening?" — that is induction (and it produced the concept of regression to the mean).

The cycle (Bhattacherjee, 2012) — a favourite diagram question

Induction and deduction are not rivals — they form a loop:

Observations→ INDUCTIVE →Theory→ DEDUCTIVE →Hypotheses / predictions→New observations→ back to theory

This cyclical process helps improve a theory. Induction builds it; deduction tests it; the test throws up new observations; those refine the theory again.

3.4 Ontology and Epistemology high yield

The two words you must define precisely

Ontology = your assumption about the nature of reality — what we think reality looks like and how we view the world.

Epistemology = your assumption about how we can know that reality — what counts as knowledge.

Why it matters

"Researchers can follow the same steps and still design completely different studies — because their ontology and epistemology differ."

Someone who believes there is one objective social reality out there (realist ontology) and that we know it through measurement (positivist epistemology) will run a survey. Someone who believes reality is socially constructed and multiple (relativist/constructivist ontology) and is known through people's interpretations (interpretivist epistemology) will run interviews. Same topic, different study.

The choice of research approach is shaped by three things: Ontology, Epistemology, and the Research Goal.

3.5 Understanding 'Qualitative' Research

Definition (Hennink et al., 2015)

Qualitative research allows one to examine people's experience in detail using research methods such as in-depth interviews (IDIs), focus group discussions (FGDs), observations, life histories and ethnography.

Approach: identify issues from the perspective of the study participants and understand the meanings and interpretations they give to their behaviour — this is the interpretive approach.

Embracing subjectivity (Yardley, 2024)

  • "People don't behave like matter — solid, liquid, or gases." The human experience is rich and diverse.
  • Qualitative research acknowledges the subjective nature of human experience.
  • That subjective nature is coloured by personal beliefs, values, attitudes, feelings and experiences.
  • Subjectivity has value — it is not a defect to be eliminated.
Emic vs Etic memorise

Emic = the insiders' perspective. Etic = the outsiders' perspective. Qualitative research is emic.

The classroom example: a household has a toilet, but the women do not use it.

  • Etic (outsider) reading: "They are ignorant / lack awareness / the behaviour-change campaign failed."
  • Emic (insider) reading: the toilet has no water supply; using it is considered polluting to the home; there is no privacy/lock; going to the fields is a social occasion with other women; the structure is used for storage. Only the insider's account explains the behaviour.

Applications of qualitative research (Hennink et al., 2015)

Qualitative research is used to:

  • understand behaviour, beliefs, opinions and emotions from the perspective of study participants themselves;
  • understand and explain people's views and behaviour;
  • understand processes — such as how people make decisions, negotiate a job, or manage a business;
  • uncover the meaning that people give to their experiences;
  • understand social interactions among people and the norms and values they share;
  • identify the social, cultural, economic or physical context in which activities take place;
  • give voice to the issues of a certain study population;
  • provide depth, detail, nuance and context;
  • examine sensitive issues in detail — sexuality, violence, personal relationships;
  • study complex issues — human trafficking, drug use — which are too complex or hidden to be easily disentangled by quantitative research.

Also: exploring new topics, explaining beliefs and behaviour, and identifying the cultural norms of a society.

What qualitative research is good at answering

  • Why people behave the way they do
  • How opinions and attitudes are formed
  • How people are affected by the events going on around them
  • How and why cultures have developed
  • The differences between social groups

When to choose qualitative: when you want to deeply understand something; when you are interested in the lived experiences of a particular group and want to highlight those experiences, not just generalize about them.

What the qualitative researcher must be

  • Embrace and understand the contextual influence on the research issue.
  • Be open-minded, curious, empathic, flexible, and a thorough listener.
  • Be transparent about the method.
  • Ask very open questions and give the participant all the space to talk.
  • Use rapport building to create a comfortable atmosphere in which participants can disclose sensitive things.

3.6 Validity and reliability in qualitative research high yield

The core reframing (Yardley, 2024)

In qualitative work, validity and reliability become the authenticity / trustworthiness of the information — i.e. credibility — and being transparent about how one found it, or showing how one did it — i.e. dependability.

04Essentials of Literature Review and Research Writing

Session 4. Why you read before you write, how to handle sources honestly, and how to write research that people can actually follow.

4.1 Why do a literature review? (Kumar, 2014; Bryman, 2008)

  1. To place your findings in the context of the body of knowledge.
  2. Not to reinvent the wheel — avoid duplicating concepts.
  3. To compare your findings with others'.
  4. To broaden the knowledge base — including resolving inconsistencies in the existing literature.
  5. To establish the theoretical and conceptual framework for your study.
  6. To justify your selection of research questions — by finding gaps and neglected aspects.
  7. To become conversant with the methods used in your field.
'Literature review' vs 'summary of literature' likely 2-marker

A summary of literature lists what each study said, one after another ("A found X. B found Y. C found Z."). It is a catalogue.

A literature review synthesises, organises by theme, compares, contrasts, identifies patterns, resolves inconsistencies, and argues towards a gap that your study will fill. It has a direction and a conclusion.

The governing principle: "The whole is more than the sum of its parts."

Can reviews contribute to original research?

  • Reviews are catalogues of original findings but not original by themselves — and writers shy away from considering a review at par with original research.
  • But: "the whole is more than the sum of its parts". Reviews can give new insights, particularly through the interdisciplinary acumen of the authors.
  • A review offers the scope for viewing an issue through a kaleidoscope of multiple disciplines.
  • Standalone literature review papers are a legitimate publication type.
The Kanizsa illusion — why this was shown in class

The Kanizsa triangle and Kanizsa square: three or four 'pac-man' shapes arranged so that you clearly perceive a bright triangle or square that is not actually drawn. The edges exist only in your perception of the whole.

The point: individually, each source (each pac-man) shows you nothing. Assembled and synthesised, a shape appears that no single study contained. That emergent shape is what a good literature review produces — it is where a review becomes original.

4.2 Always go to the original source of data — the Kuznets case favourite example

Rule: it is always advisable to go to the original source of data, as far as possible. The Kuznets curve is the course's extended cautionary tale.

StageWhat happened
The hypothesisKuznets (1955) proposed the growth–inequality relationship: as an economy develops (agriculture → industry, greater urbanization of the labour force), inequality first rises and then falls — the inverted-U "Kuznets curve". Inequality measured via the Gini coefficient; the implication drawn by others was that a free market will deliver prosperity and equity.
What Kuznets himself saidIn the same paper (1955, p.26) he wrote: "I am acutely conscious of the meagreness of reliable information presented. The paper is perhaps 5 per cent empirical information and 95 per cent speculation, some of it possibly tainted by wishful thinking." He repeatedly warned the data were little more than "pure guesswork", and that even if valid they pertained to "an extremely limited period of time and to exceptional historical experiences."
What the field didThe warnings were ignored. The Kuznets curve was "raised to the level of law" by other economists (Fogel, 1987).
The correctionPiketty (2013), using the top decile's share of US national income, 1910–2010, argues Kuznets mistook the 1930–1950 decrease in inequality for the endpoint of its development. Since 1950 inequality has again reached pre-WWII levels; similar trends are visible across European countries.
The moral, in one line

If you cite a secondary account, you inherit its distortions. Had researchers read Kuznets's own paper, a speculative hypothesis would never have become an economic "law". Always go to the original.

4.4 Referencing, data care, authorship and plagiarism

Referencing

  • Attribute data to its reference.
  • Cite in text as (author, year).
  • Use footnotes where appropriate.
  • Maintain a proper reference section; follow the journal guideline.
  • Keep a file-naming convention; use Google Scholar for retrieval and citation export.

Data care

  • Care in data collection and in sharing data (ethical aspects).
  • No Type I and Type II errors — do not claim an effect that isn't there (Type I / false positive), and do not miss one that is (Type II / false negative).

Authorship care

  • Authorship should be based on contribution.
  • No honorary authorship, no gift authorship.
  • On a multi-authored paper, be aware of all parts of the work — you are answerable for all of it.
  • The contribution of each author must be stated clearly.

Plagiarism

  • It is a serious issue. "You know it."
  • Plagiarism software exists — check with the library.
  • The onus lies with the authors — not the reviewers, not the software.
  • Even very senior scientists have been caught (the C.N.R. Rao case was cited) — seniority is no protection.
  • Best strategy: read, understand, close the source, and write it in your own words — then cite. Never paraphrase with the source open in front of you.

4.5 Presentation of data and findings

Tabulation

  • Tables must be self-explanatory — readable without the surrounding text.
  • Table footnotes need to be placed as Notes.
  • Optimize the information and optimize the entries — do not dump every cross-tabulation you produced.
The table shown in class — "Community responses on 'company is an asset to our community'"

A 5-point scale (strongly agree → strongly disagree) broken down by total, marital status, gender, age (four age bands), all in percentages, N=271. The shortcoming to identify: far too many disaggregations in one table for the reader to extract a message; no clear takeaway; percentages without base counts per column; the reader has to do the analysis themselves. Optimise entries — show the cut that carries the finding.

Charts

Correctness above all — the chart type must match the data, axes must be honest, and the visual must not imply a pattern the data does not support.

Presentation of findings

Follow Nature's "Here we show…" convention — state plainly what your study found.

  • Report findings from your own study, not generic conclusions.
  • Report trends.
  • Report unexpected findings.
  • Report data that reinforces or contradicts other data.
  • Report extreme values.
  • Report data that raises questions.
  • Present data point-wise on certain occasions.

Nuances of the write-up

  • Radical recommendations require greater explanation.
  • Heading length — not too long, not too short.
  • Number of sections/sub-sections — not too many, not too few.
  • Section length — not too long, not too short.
  • PowerPoint is indicative, not the report.
  • Read your own report imagining you are the consumer of it.

Overall

  • Have an imagination of the final product before you start.
  • Make your analysis apparent.
  • In a long report, bring the important points upfront.
  • Provide a hook — e.g. the main points in the executive summary.
  • Give it time.
  • Acknowledge all from whom you have benefited.

05Formulating a Research Design

Session 5. Sampling in qualitative research, saturation, and the four classic qualitative research designs.

5.1 Foundational similarities and differences (recap, formalised)

Similarities: both approaches are systematic, with established protocols designed to detect patterns; both are built on empirical evidence / observable reality — what can be observed, experienced, documented or otherwise encountered in the social world.

Difference: quantitative represents human experience in numerical categories; qualitative gives detailed description and analysis of the quality — the substance — of the human experience. The major difference stems from the researcher's underlying strategy: quantitative is confirmatory and deductive (general→specific, top-down, breadth); qualitative is exploratory and inductive (specific→general, bottom-up, depth).

5.2 The three descriptions of poverty — the class exercise

Three accounts of the same phenomenon were compared. This is the single clearest illustration of what qualitative data adds, and it is very likely to appear as an "explain with an example" prompt.

VersionTypeThe text
1Quantitative"Rural poverty in Tanzania has been halved in the period from 1985 to 2001. At present about 38 percent of people living in rural areas are classified as poor. This progress is reflected in the UNDP's Human Development Index for Tanzania, which rose from 0.3 in 1991 to 0.4 in 2002."
2Visual / photographicAn image of poverty — conveying material conditions directly, without numbers or words.
3Qualitative (voices)• "Poverty is like living in jail, living under bondage, waiting to be free." (a saying from Jamaica)

How to answer the three questions asked in class

Q1. How did each describe/explain poverty? (1) as a measurable rate — a headcount ratio and an index, trackable over time and comparable across countries; (2) as a visible material condition; (3) as a lived experience — powerlessness, dependence, humiliation, shame, loss of social worth.

Q2. What made 2 and 3 different from 1? Version 1 tells you how much and how many but nothing about what poverty is for the person living it. Versions 2 and 3 carry meaning, emotion, context, and the insider's (emic) definition — poverty as unfreedom and loss of dignity, not as a number below a line. They also reveal dimensions the HDI does not measure (shame, fear, dependence, social exclusion).

Q3. Which is most convincing? The expected answer is not "the third". It is: they answer different questions and are strongest together. Version 1 is essential for tracking progress, allocating budgets and comparing regions; version 3 is essential for designing an intervention that addresses what people actually experience. A policy built on (1) alone may halve a headcount without touching humiliation or powerlessness.

5.4 The four types of qualitative sampling guaranteed question

1. Convenience sampling

  • Selection is based on who is most accessible (most convenient).
  • May result in poor quality data and lack intellectual credibility.
  • Useful for conducting pilot studies.
  • Used under time/budget constraints.

2. Judgement sampling (= purposive / purposeful)

  • The idea is to select the most 'productive' sample to answer the research question(s).
  • It is more than a simple demographic framework based on age, gender, socio-economic status.
  • It may include other characteristics such as position in the household, engaging in a particular occupation, or experience of a specific event or situation.

This is the default sampling strategy in most qualitative studies — including, almost certainly, your PRA/VFS fieldwork.

3. Theoretical sampling

  • It is theory-driven rather than data-driven.
  • Primarily associated with the grounded theory research design.
  • Participants are selected progressively as the study develops, rather than all being decided at the beginning.
  • Sampling is guided by emerging concepts and categories from the data: the question you keep asking is "Who or what should I study next to refine this?"
Collect data→Analyse→Identify emerging concept→Select next participant→Collect more data→Refine concept↺

4. Snowball sampling

  • Relies on existing participants to refer new subjects, allowing the researcher to build a larger sample over time.
  • Useful for studying specific, often hard-to-reach populations, or individuals with unique experiences.
  • Can foster trust among participants, as referrals come from acquaintances — which may encourage greater willingness to participate.
Mnemonic

C-J-T-S: Convenient (whoever's there) · Judgement (whoever's most useful) · Theoretical (whoever the emerging theory demands next) · Snowball (whoever the last person knows).

5.5 How big should the sample be? — Saturation

The answer, and the concept

Marshall (1996, p.523): "…an appropriate sample size for a qualitative study is one that adequately answers the research question."

The operational criterion is Theoretical Saturation (or Data Saturation), which is reached when:

  1. No new or relevant data or themes seem to emerge; and
  2. The emerging categories and relationships are sufficiently developed to support the theoretical explanation.

Note: qualitative sample size is NOT fixed in advance the way a quantitative sample size is calculated in advance. You stop when you stop learning.

Bracketing — define this precisely likely 2-marker

Bracketing means putting aside your preconceived notions and beliefs about the phenomenon under consideration, to keep them from biasing your observations. It belongs to phenomenology.

Contrast with reflexivity (Module 8): bracketing tries to set aside the researcher's self; reflexivity examines and declares it. Know the difference — they are easy to confuse.

  • Grounded theory → I want to build a theory of a process.
  • Phenomenology → I want the meaning of an experience as lived.

  • Action research → I want to solve a problem with the community as co-researchers.

6.3 Key terminologies: Key Informants and Gatekeepers

Two terms that are frequently confused likely 2-marker

Key Informants are generally people who are associated and involved with the participants — they could also be experts. They act as the researcher's advisors and supporters throughout the study, and you must work towards establishing a trusting relationship with them.
Village examples: the schoolteacher, the ASHA/Anganwadi worker, a retired postmaster, an active SHG leader, the village elder who knows the history.

Gatekeepers are people who hold some type of authority or control over access to one or more other individuals. Because they have the power to give or deny access to the population of interest, you may need to make a serious effort to develop a strong, positive relationship with them.
Village examples: the sarpanch, the panchayat secretary, an NGO field coordinator, a factory manager, a husband/mother-in-law controlling access to women respondents, a school principal.

The distinction in one line: a key informant gives you information; a gatekeeper gives you access. One person can be both.

Requirements of a qualitative interviewer

  • Listening — hear, digest and comprehend the participant's answers in order to decide how to probe further.
  • A clear and logical mind — think quickly to distil the essential points of what the participant is saying, exercise judgement about what to pursue, and simultaneously formulate relevant follow-up questions.
  • A good memory — to make a mental note of a point and return to it at a judicious moment to seek further clarification or elaboration.
  • Establish good rapport — a good working relationship with people from all walks of life: powerful, marginalised, vulnerable; put the participant at ease and create a climate of trust; have a real desire to understand from the interviewee's perspective.

6.5 Focus Group Discussions (FGDs)

Definition memorise

An FGD is "a discussion conducted by a trained moderator among a small group of respondents in an unstructured and natural manner."

Break the name down: "Focus" = focused on the topic; "Group" = a group having certain characteristics — it should be homogeneous in demographic and socio-economic characteristics.

The defining feature: data are generated by the INTERACTION BETWEEN GROUP PARTICIPANTS. This is what distinguishes an FGD from a set of simultaneous interviews.

  • Participants present their own views and experience, but they also hear from other people — they listen, reflect on what is said, and in the light of this consider their own standpoint further.
  • The value of the technique lies in discovering unexpected findings, often obtained from a free-flowing group discussion.
  • There is a range of stimuli — which may come from other group members or from the moderator.

6.6 Observation

Definition

'Observation' involves situating what we see in relation to what we know about a particular setting.

In qualitative research, observational data is as important as interview data. The researcher might use interviews to make sense of what they observe, and observation to interpret their interview data — this is triangulation.

What to observe?

  • An understanding of the physical, social, cultural and economic contexts.
  • Social relationships among and between people.
  • Ideas, norms and events.
  • People's behaviours and activities — what they do, how frequently, and with whom.
A worked observation checklist — 'Culture' (from the class handout)
  • What festivals are celebrated in the village?
  • Community functions, gatherings, etc.
  • Are festivals celebrated at the whole-village level / specific community levels / only specific household levels? Gather experiences relating to these.
  • Does the village participate in social functions outside the village, or do outsiders (neighbouring villages) participate in social functions here?
  • Are these events linked to cycles of production (sowing, harvest) or of life (birth, marriage, death)? Keep an eye on expenses incurred during such events and their impact on household finances.
  • Are there any exploitative practices involved? Do these cultural practices reinforce hierarchies, or are they breaking those inequalities?

Notice the structure: descriptive questions first, then who is included/excluded, then economic consequence, then power. Use this pattern for any observation theme.

The three criteria observation must meet memorise
  1. It must serve a specific research purpose — the 'data' should be related to your research question/theme and/or your theoretical framework.
  2. The observation should be systematically planned and carried out.
  3. The observation must be recorded systematically.

How to record observation data

  • Data consist of the detailed field notes that the researcher records in a field diary — make careful, objective notes.
  • Although typically textual, such data may also include maps and other diagrams, such as kinship or organizational charts.
  • Occasionally it may involve quantification of something and therefore produce numerical data (e.g. counts of how many households have a particular feature, frequency of an activity).
Participant observation

The aim is "to learn what life is like for an 'insider' while remaining, inevitably, an 'outsider'" — to experience and observe at first hand social actions, behaviour, interactions, relationships and events.

Example: someone studying the behaviour of people on public transportation may spend hours riding the subway in an attempt to gain perspective.

7.1 What is data analysis? And what types exist?

Definition

Data analysis is "connecting the dots" — telling your audience how the pieces of information fit together. It means searching across a data set — be that a number of interviews or focus groups, or a range of observations — to find repeated patterns of meaning.

Always note: triangulation — cross-checking findings across different data sources and methods.

Types of qualitative analysis

TypeWhat it examines
Narrative analysisThe meaning of respondents' lives — i.e. how they interpret their world — and the ways in which they articulate or voice their experiences — i.e. how they tell their stories, and from what position. Moves from the narrative of individuals → a larger common narrative.
Case-study analysisGoes deeply into the experience of one individual (or one unit/case).
Discourse analysisHow language and communication are used in real-life social context to shape meaning, power and identity.
Ethnographic accountsLargely descriptive accounts that detail the way of life of particular individuals, groups or organisations.
Thematic analysisThe foundational method — identify and interpret patterns or themes across a data set. (Detailed below.)
What all forms of qualitative analysis have in common

Huberman and Miles (1994): all forms of qualitative analysis are based on three procedures:

  1. Data reduction
  2. Data display
  3. Conclusion: drawing / verifying

And all analysis seeks to identify patterns, processes, structures, meanings, and underlying themes in qualitative data.

7.2 Thematic analysis and 'thick description'

Definition

Thematic analysis is a foundational method for qualitative analysis — "a research method used to identify and interpret patterns or themes in a data set; it often leads to new insights and understanding." It comprises 'thick descriptive data'.

Thick description — Clifford Geertz likely 2–5 marker

Thin description merely reports facts. It is insufficient and potentially misleading.

Thick description goes beyond facts to include:

  • Context
  • Commentary
  • Interpretation

Purpose: culture cannot be understood through factual description alone.

Geertz's classic illustration: a rapid contraction of the right eyelid. Thin: "the eyelid contracted." Thick: was it an involuntary twitch, a conspiratorial wink, a parody of someone else's wink, or a rehearsal of that parody? Identical physical fact — four completely different social meanings. Only context, commentary and interpretation can tell them apart.

7.3 The six-step thematic analysis technique THE most likely long-answer question

1 · Transcription & familiarisation
(select quotations)→ 2 · Keywords→ 3 · Coding→ 4 · Themes→ 5 · Conceptualization→ 6 · Conceptual model

Step 1 — Transcription, familiarization with the data, and selection of quotations

  • It involves the transcription of data and familiarizing oneself with it.
  • Produce a rigorous and thorough transcript — a 'verbatim' account of all verbal (and sometimes non-verbal) utterances.
  • Immerse yourself in the data to the extent that you are familiar with the depth and breadth of the content.
  • Do repeated 'active' reading of the data — searching for meanings, patterns and so on.
  • It is ideal to read through the entire data set at least once before you begin coding.
  • Identify important sections — select quotes that bring the data to life.
Rules for using quotations examinable list
  • Maintain confidentiality.
  • Quotes have the potential to animate the data and provide a vivid portrayal of the subject matter.
  • They enhance the reader's engagement with the text and underscore unique aspects of the data.
  • Incorporate a diverse range of participants' voices — 'inclusive representation' (don't quote the same articulate respondent five times).
  • Provide adequate context so readers can accurately comprehend the essence of a quote.
  • Quotes may serve various purposes: evidence, explanation, illustration, impression, representation, and readability enhancement.

Step 2 — Selection of keywords (prior to coding)

  • Start taking notes or marking ideas for coding (keywords) that you will return to in later phases. This is a form of initial coding.
  • The selection of powerful, meaningful and pertinent words from quotations as keywords paves the way for subsequent code development and data interpretation.

How to select keywords — five criteria:

  1. They capture the genuine experiences and perceptions of the participants.
  2. They are rich in meaning and give a detailed understanding of the phenomenon being studied.
  3. They frequently occur in the data — signalling significance and relevance.
  4. They relate to the theoretical foundation of the research.
  5. They are words central to understanding the phenomenon under study.

Step 3 — Coding

Definition

Coding is the process of cataloguing information: short phrases or words are assigned to segments of data that capture the data's core message, significance, or theme. This step simplifies complex textual data.

A codebook is a list of codes and their descriptions.

Note: your coded data differ from your units of analysis (your themes), which are (often) broader.

Key advice for this phase:

  • Code for as many potential themes/patterns as possible (time permitting) — you never know what might be interesting later.
  • Code extracts of data inclusively — i.e. keep a little of the surrounding data if relevant.
  • Remember you can code individual extracts in as many different 'themes' as they fit into — so an extract may be uncoded, coded once, or coded many times, as relevant. (Multiple meanings.)

How to select codes — five criteria:

  1. It represents the data in a meaningful way — encapsulating various aspects of the data.
  2. Create codes based on keywords, reflecting the relationship between facts and theories.
  3. Codes should be inferential and descriptive, and closely related to the RQ.
  4. Codes should be unique and well-defined — to prevent overlaps — and should also include outliers.
  5. Codes should logically fit and align with the purpose of your research.

Step 4 — Theme development (categorization)

  • Begins when all data have been coded and collated and you have a long list of different codes — i.e. the codebook is prepared.
  • It re-focuses the analysis at the broader level of themes — categorising the different codes into potential themes.
  • The question to keep asking: how do different codes combine to form an overarching theme?

How to select themes — four criteria:

  1. They highlight the interrelationship between various codes.
  2. They reflect the recurring patterns identified in the data.
  3. They align with and address the researcher's aims and objectives.
  4. They contribute to developing a story that helps understand the studied phenomenon.

Step 5 — Conceptualization through interpretation of themes

  • For each individual theme, conduct and write a detailed analysis.
  • Identify the 'story' that each theme tells.
  • Work out how it fits into the broader overall 'story' you are telling about your data.
  • Consider each theme in relation to the others.
  • You can have sub-themes — essentially themes-within-a-theme — useful for giving structure to a particularly large and complex theme.
  • This is also the point to start thinking about the names you will give them in the final analysis.

One worked example running through all three

Raw quote: "The hospital is 40 km away. I have to ask my son to take me on the bike, and he loses a day's wage. So I wait until I really cannot bear it."

  • Keywords: "40 km", "ask my son", "loses a day's wage", "wait".
  • Codes: distance-to-facility, dependence-on-male-relative-for-mobility, opportunity-cost-of-care, delayed-care-seeking.
  • Theme: "Care is rationed by the household, not by the patient" — combining the mobility, cost and delay codes into an argument that answers the research question.

7.5 Step 6 — Development of the conceptual model

  • The final step in thematic analysis.
  • It begins when you have a set of fully worked-out themes, and involves the final analysis and write-up of the report.
  • It means creating a unique representation of the data, and is often guided by existing theories.
  • It serves to answer the research questions and underscore the study's contribution to knowledge.
Definition — conceptual framework memorise

"A conceptual framework is a visual or narrative representation of the key concepts or themes in a study and the relationships among them. It provides direction for the research process and helps researchers organize their ideas systematically."

The objective of the write-up

  • "To tell the complicated story of your data in a way that convinces the reader of the merit and validity of your analysis."
  • Your write-up must provide sufficient evidence of the themes within the data — i.e. enough data extracts to demonstrate the prevalence of the theme.
  • Go beyond description of the data, and make an argument in relation to your research question.

Example theme statement used in class: "Migration was perceived as causing increased human–wildlife conflicts." — note that it is a claim, not a topic label.

7.6 How to present your findings — the sandwich method

  • The bulk of your findings section will usually consist of quotations from your interviews or extended descriptions from field notes.
  • It should be organized by theme, rather than being a random list of quotes or observations.

Layout of the findings section

  • Break the findings/results section into subsections, with each theme explicitly noted in its subsection title.
  • Within each subsection: describe the overall patterns in the data related to that theme, then give specific cases that back your interpretations of what you observed or what your interviewees said — i.e. provide evidence to back up your themes.
The sandwich method guaranteed question — know the three layers
LayerWhat goes in it
Top bun
(analytical level)		The analytical point — highlighting a pattern in the data. You state the claim first.
The patty
(empirical level)		The empirical evidence for that pattern — the quote (interview/FGD) or the observation (field note). Precede it with a descriptive account of the participant: pseudonym, context, position in the village.
Bottom bun
(analytical level)		An analytical point giving useful context for situating your example among the many episodes you observed or people you talked to — i.e. how typical/atypical is this? Then transition to the next case — perhaps another full sandwich, or directly to the "meat" of a second example related to the first.

For interviews and FGDs: the quote is the patty, surrounded by two buns of analytical context.
For ethnographic observational data: swap the interview-based patty for an observation-based one — quoting the relevant field note, or summarising your observations in one or more sentences.

A model paragraph in sandwich form

[Top bun] Households consistently described healthcare decisions as being made by the family rather than the patient, with women's access mediated by the availability of a male escort.

[Notice] Kamlaben (pseudonym), a 52-year-old agricultural labourer from a landless household in the eastern hamlet, described this directly:

[Patty] "The hospital is 40 km away. I have to ask my son to take me on the bike, and he loses a day's wage. So I wait until I really cannot bear it."

[Bottom bun] Eleven of the fourteen women interviewed described a similar calculation, and in each case the deciding factor was not the cost of treatment but the foregone wage of the accompanying male relative. This suggests that transport subsidies alone would not change care-seeking behaviour, which frames the next theme — the gendered organisation of household mobility.

The 'Discussion' section — the step after findings

The findings section reports what you found. The Discussion then: restates the key finding, interprets it, compares it with the existing literature (agreement? contradiction? extension?), states the implications for policy/practice, acknowledges the limitations, and suggests future research. Remember from Module 4: radical recommendations require greater explanation.

8.1 Ethics in research — informed consent

Definition — informed consent memorise verbatim

Informed consent is "the knowing consent of individuals to participate as an exercise of their choice, free from any element of fraud, deceit, duress, or similar unfair inducement or manipulation."

It may be verbal or written.

How consent works in your VFS fieldwork — two levels
  1. Permission from the village leader to conduct the larger research.
  2. Individual consent when you plan to conduct formal audio-recorded interviews.

Permission at the community level does not substitute for consent at the individual level. Note the link to gatekeepers (Module 6.3) — the sarpanch grants access, but each participant grants consent.

8.2 Reflexivity and Positionality high yield — often asked together

The problem being solved: the researcher's identity can influence all aspects of research — including the research question, study design, data collection and data analysis. How do we handle that? Two answers: reflexivity and positionality.

Reflexivity

Reflexivity is "a second-order questioning and challenging of one's own thoughts and beliefs."

  • It is a process we engage in throughout the research — from conception through to dissemination.
  • Aim: producing a more trustworthy and honest account of the research.
Positionality

Positionality is "our understanding of ourselves — of who we are and what we bring to our research."

  • The positionality statement in a publication is "a summary of that understanding of ourselves, to highlight how our very being may have shaped our work."
  • Researchers must be forthright about what influenced their research and about their interpretations of social reality that are influenced by their positionality.
  • Being candid about our positionality increases the credibility of our research and provides context for users of our research.
How to write a positionality statement — what to include

Writing one helps you intentionally reflect on your identity, life history, experiences, values and the things/issues that are important to you. This reflection helps you determine what aspect of your identity is of broader sociological interest, which is useful in narrowing your research interests. Even if you already know your topic, it helps focus your research on issues important to who you are or to your political/world views. Include:

  • Identity characteristics — age, gender, sexuality, ethnicity, social class, disability status, citizenship, immigration status, religion, marital status, etc.
  • Life experiences — previous or current job, volunteering activities, membership in advocacy groups, etc.
  • Political, philosophical and theoretical beliefs — the lens through which you view and interpret the world.
  • Relationship to the phenomenon of interest — your insider and/or outsider status.

Applied to you: an IRMA student doing PRA in a village is, typically, an urban-educated outsider, younger than most respondents, associated in villagers' minds with an institution and possibly with benefits — all of which shapes what people tell you.

8.3 PRA: Participatory Rural Appraisal

RRA vs PRA classic comparison question

Rapid Rural Appraisal (RRA) is "a condensed version of PRA designed for situations where time is of the essence." It aims to gather essential information quickly, often in response to specific development challenges or opportunities. It is EXTRACTIVE — information is used as per the project staff's needs.

Participatory Rural Appraisal (PRA) represents a shift from extractive survey questionnaires (RRA) to EXPERIENCE SHARING by local people. Developed in the early 1990s, with a considerable paradigm shift from a top-down to a bottom-up approach.

One-line difference: in RRA, outsiders extract information; in PRA, local people generate, own and analyse the information.

Unpacking the name P-R-A

  • Participatory — people are involved in the process; a "bottom-up" approach.
  • Rural — though the name suggests rural, the techniques can be used in ANY situation, urban or rural, with both literate and illiterate people. (An easy exam trap — PRA is not restricted to villages.)
  • Appraisal — finding out information about problems, needs and potential in a village. It is the first stage in any project.
Robert Chambers — the name to cite memorise

Robert Chambers, Institute of Development Studies, University of Sussex, defined PRA as:

"An approach and methods for learning about rural life and conditions FROM, WITH and BY rural people."

His central question: "Whose reality counts?"

The three prepositions matter: from them (they are the source), with them (they are partners), by them (they are the analysts).

PRA is one of the most popular and effective approaches to gather information in rural areas.

8.4 The five key principles of PRA guaranteed question

PrincipleWhat it means
1. PARTICIPATIONDesigned to involve local people — not only as sources of information, but as PARTNERS in gathering and analyzing the information.
2. FLEXIBILITYThe appropriate combination of techniques is determined by: (a) the size and skill of the PRA team; (b) the time and resources available; (c) the topic and location of the work.
3. TEAMWORKLocal language; locals with a very few outsiders; a significant presence of women.
4. OPTIMAL IGNORANCETo be efficient in terms of both time and money, PRA work intends to gather JUST ENOUGH information to make the necessary recommendations and decisions — no more. (Deliberately not knowing what you don't need to know.)
5. SYSTEMATICNot statistical. Validity comes from inference from other techniques and cross-checking the findings in community meetings.
Mnemonic

P-F-T-O-S → "Please Follow These Own Steps". Or note the logic: who is involved (Participation, Teamwork), how you choose tools (Flexibility), how much you collect (Optimal ignorance), how you validate (Systematic).

8.5 Other principles of PRA, and the facilitator's role

Other principles

  • Offsetting biases
  • Reversal of roles — the local person teaches, the outsider learns
  • Rapid and progressive learning
  • Be gender sensitive
  • Focused learning — relative comparison/ranking rather than absolute measurement
  • Seeking for diversity/differences — the outlier matters
  • Be aware of biases — seek opinions from women, the poorest, the power-less
  • "Handing over the stick" — people take the lead (literally: give them the chalk/stick and let them draw the map)
  • Attitude — respect, humility, patience, and a sense of humour
Role of the PRA facilitator — cautions and attitudes very examinable list
  • Avoid using complex language/words; try to talk in the local language.
  • Keep a low profile.
  • Listen carefully and do not teach them — do not interrupt.
  • Stay neutral — do not assume the answer.
  • Treat and respect people according to local customs and culture.
  • Use secondary information to know more about the village beforehand.
  • Spell out clearly why you are doing it.
  • Show genuine interest in local issues.
  • Choose a time and venue that are convenient for local people — not for you.

1. Transect walk

The facilitator and participants walk down through the village to obtain an overall understanding of the village. It explores:

  • The spatial dimension — physical build and locations
  • Quality of habitation
  • Geographical stretches
  • Availability of resources — soil type, erosion, land use (forest, waste, grazing, cropping), water, vegetation, crops
  • Problems and priorities

2. Historical mapping / Timeline

  • To understand the historical background — how the village has evolved over time, as per the locals' perception and priorities.
  • It is a good way to build rapport.
  • Basic events, with a timeline, can be shown — events that brought immense changes in village life (a drought, the arrival of electricity/a road/a school, a flood, a factory, a migration wave).
  • Involve elders in sketching the map.
  • Note: there are different types of historical mapping (e.g. a timeline of events vs. a 'then and now' pair of maps vs. a trend line for a specific variable).

3. Social map

  • Sketching/drawing of houses and other social facilities and infrastructure — temple, stores, rice mills, school, pharmacy, trails and roads, water pumps, irrigation and recreation facilities.
  • Purpose: to visualize and situate the location of households and other social facilities/infrastructure.

How to facilitate:

  • Ask participants to draw a map of the village showing all households.
  • Discuss whether the total number of households has increased or shrunk in recent years — and why.
  • Encourage the group to show which different ethnic groups live in the village; using a common symbol, mark households where minority ethnic groups live.
  • Do the same for different religious groups.
  • Ask the group to indicate with a symbol all female-headed households — and make sure everybody has the same understanding of what the characteristics of a female-headed household are.

There are different kinds of social mapping — e.g. mapping by caste, by landholding, by service access, or by vulnerability.

4. Resource map

  • Helps us learn about a community and its resource base.
  • The primary concern is NOT to develop an accurate map, but to get useful information about LOCAL PERCEPTIONS of resources. (A very likely exam point.)

Key questions:

  • What resources are abundant or scarce?
  • Does everyone have equal access to land? (social hierarchies)
  • Where do people go to collect water? Who collects water?
  • Where do people go to collect firewood? Who collects firewood?
  • Where do people graze livestock?
  • What development activities do you carry out as a whole community? Where?
  • Which resource do you have the most problem with?

How to facilitate: use separate groups of men and women — they may use different resources. Also separate other social groups: caste / ethnicity / religion.

5. Seasonal map / calendar

  • Determines patterns and trends throughout the year.
  • Purposes: rainfall distribution, food availability, agricultural production, income and expenditures, health problems, and others.

Key questions:

  • What are the busiest months of the year?
  • At what time of year is food scarce?
  • How does income and expenditure vary over the year for men and women?
  • How does rainfall vary over the year?
  • How does water availability for human consumption vary over the year?
  • How does livestock forage availability vary over the year?
  • How does credit availability vary over the year?

How to facilitate:

  • It is usually easiest to start the calendar by asking about rainfall patterns (everyone can answer it, and it anchors the other rows).
  • Move to the next topic and ask during which months food is usually scarce — and discuss why.
  • Make sure your copy of the seasonal calendar has a KEY explaining the different items and symbols used.

Related variants shown in class: the Crop Calendar (sowing/weeding/harvest by crop across months) and the Livelihood Activity Chart (which livelihood activity is pursued in which months, by whom).

6. Time-activity chart

  • Captures the daily routine.
  • One can have such charts for different age groups and occupations — and, critically, for men and women separately, which is usually where the finding is.

7. Venn diagram (Institutional Relationship Diagram)

  • Shows institutions, organisations, groups and important individuals found in the village, as well as the villagers' view of their importance in the community.
  • It also indicates how close the contact and cooperation between those organisations and groups is.

Key questions:

  • Which organisations/institutions/groups are working in or with the community?
  • Which institutions/groups do the villagers regard as most important, and why?
  • Which groups are addressing household food security and nutrition issues?
  • Which organisations work together?
  • Are there groups meant for women or men only?
  • Are some particular groups or kinds of people excluded from membership of, or from receiving services from, certain institutions?
How to facilitate — the coding of the circles memorise this

It is good to form separate focus groups because of social hierarchies.

Circle SIZE = importance of the institution. Circle DISTANCE/OVERLAP = degree of contact and co-operation with the community:

  • Largely distanced circles → no or little contact or co-operation
  • Circles close to each other → only loose contacts exist
  • Touching circles → some co-operation
  • Overlapping circles → close co-operation

8. Pair-wise / Matrix / Preference ranking

  • Helps villagers to set priorities — problems, needs, actions, etc.
  • Ranking can be undertaken with key informants or with a group of villagers representing a good mixture of interests (old, young, men, women, etc.).
  • How it works: people are presented with a PAIR of preferences/facilities/problems and asked: "Which is preferred between the two, and why?"
  • At the end, preferences and problems can be counted and ranked.
  • A problem matrix can be made for different problems; a solution matrix can also be done for important problems.

The "and why?" is the qualitative part — the ranking gives you the order, the reasons give you the criteria people actually use.

9. Wealth ranking

  • To determine the economic attributes of households.
  • To determine the extent of poverty in the village.
  • Identify wealth indicators and categorize households into rich, average and poor.
  • The opening question: "How can we tell whether a household is relatively rich, average, or poor in this village?"
The card-sorting method (the 'Alternatively' slide) — know these steps
  1. Prepare a list of all household heads. Write their names on separate cards.
  2. Define a ranking in discussion with villagers — e.g. 1 to 5, where 1 is very poor and 5 is very rich.
  3. Ask each individual to pick a card and give it a rank.
  4. Get the average score for each card and arrange them in increasing order.
  5. The highest score is the richest; the least score is the very poorest.

KEY POINT (the examinable one): "The ranking is based on the COMMUNITY'S PERCEPTION of relative wealth, rather than on the researcher's own assessment of household income or assets." This is the emic principle (Module 3) operationalised as a tool.

Preference rankingVillagers' ranking of the problems migration causes (or of reasons for migrating)Establishes community priorities and the criteria behind them, for recommendations
Transect walkLocked houses, fallow plots, condition of habitation, new pucca houses built with remittancesProvides physical corroboration — triangulation of what the maps and discussions claim

Tie it together: the maps give who and where, the calendars give when, the ranking gives who is affected most, the Venn diagram gives what institutional support exists, and the transect walk triangulates all of it. Supplement with IDIs with left-behind women and returned migrants, and separate FGDs for men and women (social hierarchies). Analyse via thematic analysis.

Q. "Differentiate between qualitative and quantitative research." (10 marks)

Skeleton: (1) Open with the two foundational similarities — both systematic with established protocols, both built on empirical/observable evidence. (2) State the foundational difference — numerical categories vs the quality/substance of human experience. (3) Give the full comparison table from Module 3.7 (objective, purpose, data, study population, nomenclature, methods, analysis, outcome). (4) Add the strategy difference — confirmatory/deductive vs exploratory/inductive. (5) Add ontology/epistemology as the underlying cause of the divergence. (6) Close with the caution: quantitative is often perceived as better, but this rests on a misunderstanding — plus the four integration designs (Miles & Huberman). (7) Illustrate with the three descriptions of poverty.

Q. "Explain the six steps of thematic analysis with an example." (10–15 marks)

Skeleton: (1) Define thematic analysis and thick description. (2) Draw the pipeline diagram. (3) Take each step with its sub-criteria: Step 1 transcription/verbatim/active reading/quotation rules; Step 2 keywords + the 5 selection criteria; Step 3 coding + codebook + the 3 pieces of key advice + the 5 code-selection criteria; Step 4 theme development + the 4 theme-selection criteria; Step 5 conceptualization, the theme's 'story', sub-themes, naming; Step 6 conceptual model/framework definition. (4) Run one worked quote through keyword → code → theme. (5) Close with Braun & Clarke's checklist, especially #15 — themes do not just 'emerge'. (6) If marks allow, add the sandwich method for writing it up.

Q. "What is PRA? Explain its principles and tools." (15 marks)

Skeleton: (1) Chambers's definition + "Whose reality counts?" + IDS Sussex. (2) Unpack P-R-A (note: not restricted to rural). (3) RRA vs PRA — extractive vs experience-sharing; early 1990s paradigm shift. (4) The five key principles with explanation. (5) The other principles — especially handing over the stick, reversal of roles, offsetting biases, attitude. (6) The facilitator's cautions. (7) The nine tools — for each: what it is, what information, how to facilitate. (8) Close by noting PRA's link to emic knowledge and to ethics (consent, gatekeepers, giving voice to women/the poorest/the powerless).

Q. Scenario-based: "You are asked to study [X] in a village. Design the study." (15 marks)

This is the most likely applied question. Use this template:

  1. Unpack the problem — real vs ideal gap; the three circles (dominant worldview, stakeholders, personal); surface the embedded assumptions.
  2. State the research question(s) — apply O'Leary's 5 prompts, give 2–3 alternative questions by nature of enquiry, select one, run the 6-point checklist.
  3. State whether a hypothesis applies — and if it is exploratory, say so explicitly ("no hypothesis — no problem").
  4. Justify the qualitative approach — ontology/epistemology, emic perspective, and what qualitative answers that quantitative cannot here.
  5. Choose a research design — grounded theory / phenomenology / ethnography / action research, with justification.
  6. Sampling — which of the four types and why; mention saturation as the stopping rule.
  7. Data collection — which tools (IDI / FGD / observation / PRA tools) and what each yields; who the key informants and gatekeepers are; separate men's and women's groups because of social hierarchies.
  8. Ethics — informed consent at both levels, confidentiality, pseudonyms, reflexivity and your own positionality.
  9. Analysis — six-step thematic analysis; triangulation across methods.
  10. Write-up — findings organised by theme, sandwich method, then discussion.

Answering in this order shows you can connect all nine modules — that is what a 15-mark answer is testing.

Other probable long questions
  • "Is science the only way of knowing?" — Peirce's four methods, with examples and limitations.
  • "Explain the characteristics of the scientific method." — R-P-F-P, plus why replicability is problematic in qualitative work.
  • "Trace the journey from a research problem to a hypothesis." — O'Leary's five steps + checklist + when no hypothesis applies.
  • "Why is literature review important? Can a review be original research?" — the 7 reasons, review vs summary, the Kanizsa/kaleidoscope argument, the Kuznets original-source lesson.
  • "Discuss the types of qualitative sampling." — the four types + saturation.
  • "Describe the four qualitative research designs." — grounded theory, phenomenology, ethnography, action research.
  • "What makes a good in-depth interview?" — 5 key features + interviewer requirements + question-formulation rules + the four don'ts.
  • "Explain FGDs — characteristics, strengths and limitations." — definition, the characteristics table, the funnel, the four dos, strengths/limitations.
  • "Discuss observation as a method." — definition, what to observe, 3 criteria, recording, participant observation, the 5-level continuum, strengths/weaknesses.
  • "Discuss ethics in qualitative research." — informed consent, confidentiality, reflexivity, positionality, plus data care/authorship/plagiarism from Module 4.
  • "How would you write up qualitative findings?" — organise by theme, sandwich method, L-L-F rule, presentation nuances.
Exam-hall tactics
  • Name-drop precisely. "Peirce (1877)", "Popper", "O'Leary (2005)", "Braun & Clarke (2006)", "Chambers", "Geertz", "Marshall (1996)". Each correct attribution reads as mastery.
  • Draw the diagrams. The three-circle unpacked problem, the induction–deduction loop, the six-step TA pipeline, the FGD funnel, the PRA Venn diagram, the sandwich. They are fast marks and break up a wall of text.
  • Always give the course's own example, not a generic one — the toilet-not-used example, the recycling questions, the Tanzania/Jamaica poverty texts, Kuznets, SLS.
  • Structure every long answer: definition → why it matters → the list/table → an example → a limitation or caution. The caution at the end is what separates a good answer from a complete one.
  • If the question says "with reference to community research", bring it back to the village: gatekeepers, gendered FGDs, local language, consent from the village leader plus the individual.

HypothesisA logical conjecture (educated guess) about the relationship between two or more variables, stated testably.

Interview scheduleA set of questions designed to be asked exactly as worded; used in structured, mainly quantitative interviews.
Judgement (purposive) samplingSelecting the most productive sample for answering the research question, using characteristics beyond simple demographics.
Key informantA person associated with the participants (or an expert) who acts as the researcher's advisor and supporter and provides information.
KeywordA specific, literal word or term appearing directly in the text; used to index, search or measure frequency.
Leading questionA question phrased so as to influence the answer; to be avoided — can be neutralised by adding "…or not?".
Narrative analysisAnalysis of the meaning respondents give their lives and how they voice their experiences — from individual narratives to a larger common narrative.

ParsimonyPreferring the simplest explanation that adequately fits the evidence; how much is explained with how few variables (Ockham).
Participant observationLearning what life is like for an insider while remaining, inevitably, an outsider — experiencing and observing at first hand.
PhenomenologyStudy of lived experiences and the meaning they hold for those involved; requires dwelling with the descriptions, and bracketing.
PositionalityOur understanding of who we are and what we bring to our research, declared in a positionality statement.

PrecisionDefining concepts, variables, instruments, scales and conclusions exactly.
ProbeA follow-up intervention used to achieve depth — penetration, exploration and explanation — yielding 'explanatory evidence'.
ReflexivitySecond-order questioning and challenging of one's own thoughts and beliefs, throughout the research.
ReplicabilityOthers should be able to repeat the study and obtain the same/similar result under the same conditions.
Research designA blueprint/framework for conducting a study — the overall approach and methods for collecting and analysing data.
Research problemA gap between what is real and what is ideal/desired, which the research must bridge.
RRARapid Rural Appraisal — a condensed, time-pressed, extractive version of PRA; information used as per project staff needs.
Saturation (theoretical/data)The point at which no new or relevant data/themes emerge and categories are sufficiently developed to support the theoretical explanation.
Snowball samplingExisting participants refer new ones; useful for hard-to-reach populations; fosters trust through acquaintance referrals.
Social cuesVerbal and non-verbal signals — facial expressions, body posture, tone of voice — used to share feelings and guide conversation.
TenacityAccepting belief through familiarity and repetition — "we have always known it to be this way".
ThemeA broad interpretive construct combining multiple related codes to tell a larger, coherent story about the data.
Theoretical samplingTheory-driven, progressive selection of participants guided by concepts emerging from ongoing analysis (grounded theory).
Thick descriptionDescription going beyond facts to include context, commentary and interpretation (Geertz).
Thematic analysisA foundational qualitative method for identifying and interpreting patterns/themes in a data set.
Transect walkPRA tool: walking through the village with participants to understand its spatial dimension, habitation quality, resources, problems and priorities.
